23.3 Jordan Decomposition

Published

October 26, 2026

Motivation (Matrix case): Let \(x \in \mathrm{GL}_n(\bar{k})\). The Jordan normal form of \(x\) is given by: \[ x = s^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} B_{a_1}^{n_1} & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & B_{a_r}^{n_r} \end{pmatrix} s, \] where each \(B_{a_i}^{n_i}\) is a Jordan block of size \(n_i \times n_i\) with eigenvalue \(a_i\): \[ B_{a_i}^{n_i} = \begin{pmatrix} a_i & 1 & & \\ & a_i & \ddots & \\ & & \ddots & 1 \\ & & & a_i \end{pmatrix}. \] Define: \[ x_s := s^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} a_1 I_{n_1} & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & a_r I_{n_r} \end{pmatrix} s \in \mathrm{GL}_n(\bar{k}) \quad (\text{semisimple element}), \] \[ x_u := s^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} a_1^{-1} B_{a_1}^{n_1} & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & a_r^{-1} B_{a_r}^{n_r} \end{pmatrix} s \in \mathrm{GL}_n(\bar{k}) \quad (\text{unipotent element}). \] Then: \[ x = x_s x_u = x_u x_s \] is called the Jordan decomposition of \(x\).

Generalization:

  • Replace the algebraically closed field \(\bar{k}\) with an arbitrary perfect field \(k\).

  • Replace \(\mathrm{GL}_n\) with an arbitrary linear algebraic group \(G\). :::

Definition 1 Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space over \(k\), and let \(\alpha \in \mathrm{GL}_V(k) = \mathrm{GL}_n(k)\). Let \(P_\alpha(T) := \det(T \cdot \mathrm{id} - \alpha) \in k[T]\) be the characteristic polynomial of \(\alpha\).

  1. The eigenvalues of \(\alpha\) lie in \(k\) if \(P_\alpha(T)\) splits over \(k\): \[ P_\alpha(T) = (T - a_1)^{m_1} \cdots (T - a_r)^{m_r}, \quad a_i \in k. \]

  2. For each eigenvalue \(a \in k\) of \(\alpha\), the primary space (generalized eigenspace) is defined by: \[ V^{(a)} := \{ v \in V \mid (\alpha - a \cdot \mathrm{id})^N v = 0 \text{ for some } N \ge 1 \}. \] Then \(V = \bigoplus_{i=1}^r V^{(a_i)}\), and the usual Jordan decomposition works on each subspace.

  3. An element \(\alpha\) is semisimple if \(\alpha_{\bar{k}} \in \operatorname{End}_{\bar{k}}(V_{\bar{k}})\) is diagonalizable.

  4. An element \(\alpha\) is unipotent if there exists an integer \(N \ge 1\) such that \((\alpha - \mathrm{id})^N = 0\).

Theorem 1 Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space over a perfect field \(k\). Let \(\alpha \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(V) = \mathrm{GL}_V(k)\). Then there exist unique elements \(\alpha_s, \alpha_u \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(V)\) such that:

  1. \(\alpha = \alpha_s \alpha_u = \alpha_u \alpha_s\) (equivalently, \(\alpha = \alpha_s + \alpha_s(\alpha_u - \mathrm{id})\), where \(\alpha_s(\alpha_u - \mathrm{id})\) is nilpotent);

  2. \(\alpha_s\) is semisimple, and \(\alpha_u\) is unipotent.

In this case, \(\alpha_s\) and \(\alpha_u\) are polynomials in \(\alpha\), i.e., \(\alpha_s, \alpha_u \in k[\alpha]\).

Proof. Case 1: All eigenvalues of \(\alpha\) lie in \(k\).

The characteristic polynomial splits over \(k\): \[ P_\alpha(T) = \prod_{i=1}^r (T - a_i)^{m_i}, \quad a_i \in k. \] Then \(V = \bigoplus_{i=1}^r V^{(a_i)}\), where \(V^{(a_i)} = \ker(\alpha - a_i \cdot \mathrm{id})^{m_i}\).

Define \(\alpha_s \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(V)\) by: \[ \alpha_s\big|_{V^{(a_i)}} := a_i \cdot \mathrm{id}_{V^{(a_i)}}. \] Define \(\alpha_u := \alpha \alpha_s^{-1}\). For each \(v_i \in V^{(a_i)}\), we have: \[ (\alpha_u - \mathrm{id})^{m_i}(v_i) = (a_i^{-1} \alpha - \mathrm{id})^{m_i}(v_i) = a_i^{-m_i} (\alpha - a_i \cdot \mathrm{id})^{m_i}(v_i) = 0. \] Hence \(\alpha_u\) is unipotent.

To find a polynomial \(Q(T) \in k[T]\) such that \(Q(\alpha) = \alpha_s\), it suffices to require: \[ \big(Q(\alpha) - a_i \cdot \mathrm{id}\big)\big|_{V^{(a_i)}} = 0 \quad \text{for all } 1 \le i \le r, \] which is guaranteed if: \[ (T - a_i)^{m_i} \mid \big(Q(T) - a_i\big), \quad \text{i.e., } Q(T) \equiv a_i \pmod{(T - a_i)^{m_i}}. \] By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, such a polynomial \(Q(T)\) exists.

Similarly, there exists a polynomial \(\widetilde{Q}(T) \in k[T]\) such that \(\widetilde{Q}(\alpha) = \alpha_s^{-1}\). Then: \[ \alpha_u = \alpha \alpha_s^{-1} = \alpha \widetilde{Q}(\alpha) \] is also a polynomial in \(\alpha\).

Since \(\alpha_s, \alpha_u \in k[\alpha]\), they commute: \(\alpha_s \alpha_u = \alpha_u \alpha_s\). Moreover, any endomorphism \(\beta\) commuting with \(\alpha\) also commutes with \(\alpha_s\) and \(\alpha_u\).

Uniqueness: If \(\alpha = \alpha_s \alpha_u = \beta_s \beta_u\) is another decomposition with commuting factors in \(k[\alpha]\), then: \[ \beta_s^{-1} \alpha_s = \beta_u \alpha_u^{-1} \in k[\alpha]. \] Because \(\beta_s\) and \(\alpha_s\) commute, \(\beta_s^{-1} \alpha_s\) is semisimple. Because \(\beta_u\) and \(\alpha_u\) commute, \(\beta_u \alpha_u^{-1}\) is unipotent (all eigenvalues are \(1\)). The only endomorphism that is simultaneously semisimple and unipotent is the identity. Thus: \[ \beta_s^{-1} \alpha_s = \beta_u \alpha_u^{-1} = \mathrm{id} \implies \alpha_s = \beta_s, \quad \alpha_u = \beta_u. \]

Case 2: General case over a perfect field \(k\).

Since \(k\) is perfect, there exists a finite Galois extension \(k'/k\) such that all eigenvalues of \(\alpha\) lie in \(k'\). Let \(A := \alpha_{k'} \in \operatorname{Aut}_{k'}(V_{k'})\). By Case 1, \(A\) admits a unique Jordan decomposition: \[ A = A_s A_u = A_u A_s. \] For every \(\sigma \in \operatorname{Gal}(k'/k)\), applying \(\sigma\) yields: \[ A = \sigma(A) = \sigma(A_s) \sigma(A_u). \] Observe that:

  1. \(\sigma(A_s)\) is semisimple, and \(\sigma(A_u)\) is unipotent;

  2. \(\sigma(A_s) \sigma(A_u) = \sigma(A_u) \sigma(A_s)\).

By the uniqueness of the Jordan decomposition of \(A\) over \(k'\), we have: \[ \sigma(A_s) = A_s \quad \text{and} \quad \sigma(A_u) = A_u \quad \text{for all } \sigma \in \operatorname{Gal}(k'/k). \] By Galois descent, \(A_s\) and \(A_u\) descend to \(k\), giving \(\alpha_s, \alpha_u \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(V)\) such that \((\alpha_s)_{k'} = A_s\) and \((\alpha_u)_{k'} = A_u\). They satisfy \(\alpha = \alpha_s \alpha_u = \alpha_u \alpha_s\), with \(\alpha_s\) semisimple and \(\alpha_u\) unipotent.

It remains to show that \(\alpha_s, \alpha_u \in k[\alpha]\). We know \(A_s \in k'[A]\). Let \(Q(T) \in k'[T]\) be a polynomial of minimal degree such that \(A_s = Q(A)\). Then \(\deg Q < \deg m_k(A)\), where \(m_k(A)\) is the minimal polynomial of \(A\) over \(k\). Writing \(Q(T) = \sum_{j=0}^r c_j T^j\) with \(c_j \in k'\), we have: \[ A_s = \sigma(A_s) = \sum_{j=0}^r \sigma(c_j) A^j \implies \big(Q - \sigma(Q)\big)(A) = 0. \] If \(\sigma(c_j) \neq c_j\) for some \(j\), this would yield a non-zero polynomial of degree strictly less than \(\deg m_k(A)\) annihilating \(A\), a contradiction. Thus \(\sigma(c_j) = c_j\) for all \(\sigma \in \operatorname{Gal}(k'/k)\) and all \(j\), which implies \(c_j \in k\). Therefore, \(Q(T) \in k[T]\), so \(\alpha_s \in k[\alpha]\). The same argument holds for \(\alpha_u\), and uniqueness follows as in Case 1.

Example 1 Let \(k\) be a nonperfect field of characteristic \(2\), so that the Frobenius map \(x \mapsto x^2\) is not surjective. Choose an element \(a \in k \setminus k^2\). Consider the matrix: \[ \alpha = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ a & 0 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathrm{GL}_2(k). \] Over \(\bar{k}\), its characteristic polynomial is \(T^2 - a = (T - \sqrt{a})^2\). Its Jordan decomposition in \(\mathrm{GL}_2(k(\sqrt{a}))\) is: \[ \alpha = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{a} & 0 \\ 0 & \sqrt{a} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}} \\ \sqrt{a} & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \] where: \[ \alpha_s = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{a} & 0 \\ 0 & \sqrt{a} \end{pmatrix} \quad (\text{semisimple}), \qquad \alpha_u = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}} \\ \sqrt{a} & 0 \end{pmatrix} \quad (\text{unipotent}). \] Since \(\sqrt{a} \notin k\), the components \(\alpha_s\) and \(\alpha_u\) cannot be defined over \(k\). Therefore, \(\alpha\) has no Jordan decomposition in \(\mathrm{GL}_2(k)\).

Proposition 1  

  1. If \(W \subseteq V\) is an \(\alpha\)-stable subspace, then \(W\) is both \(\alpha_s\)-stable and \(\alpha_u\)-stable. The restriction \(\alpha|_W = \alpha_s|_W \cdot \alpha_u|_W\) is the Jordan decomposition of \(\alpha|_W\). The same statement holds for the quotient space \(V/W\).

  2. If \(\varphi: W \to V\) is a \(k\)-linear map intertwining two operators \(\alpha_W \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(W)\) and \(\alpha_V \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(V)\), i.e., \[ \begin{CD} W @>{\varphi}>> V \\ @V{\alpha_W}VV @VV{\alpha_V}V \\ W @>{\varphi}>> V \end{CD} \] commutes, then \(\varphi\) also intertwines their semisimple and unipotent parts: \[ \begin{CD} W @>{\varphi}>> V \\ @V{(\alpha_W)_s}VV @VV{(\alpha_V)_s}V \\ W @>{\varphi}>> V \end{CD} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \begin{CD} W @>{\varphi}>> V \\ @V{(\alpha_W)_u}VV @VV{(\alpha_V)_u}V \\ W @>{\varphi}>> V \end{CD} \]

  3. If \(\alpha \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(V)\) and \(\beta \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(W)\), then: \[ (\alpha \otimes \beta)_s = \alpha_s \otimes \beta_s \quad \text{and} \quad (\alpha \otimes \beta)_u = \alpha_u \otimes \beta_u, \] which gives the Jordan decomposition: \[ \alpha \otimes \beta = (\alpha_s \otimes \beta_s)(\alpha_u \otimes \beta_u) = (\alpha_u \otimes \beta_u)(\alpha_s \otimes \beta_s). \]

Definition 2 Let \(V\) be a vector space over \(k\), and let \(\alpha: V \to V\) be a \(k\)-linear endomorphism.

  1. \(\alpha\) is locally finite if \(V\) is a union of \(\alpha\)-stable finite-dimensional subspaces: \[ V = \bigcup_{\substack{W \subseteq V, \, \dim W < \infty \\ \alpha(W) \subseteq W}} W. \]

  2. If \(\alpha\) is locally finite, then:

    • \(\alpha\) is semisimple if for every finite-dimensional \(\alpha\)-stable subspace \(W \subseteq V\), the restriction \(\alpha|_W\) is semisimple;

    • \(\alpha\) is locally unipotent if for every finite-dimensional \(\alpha\)-stable subspace \(W \subseteq V\), the restriction \(\alpha|_W\) is unipotent (the integer \(N\) such that \((\alpha|_W - \mathrm{id}_W)^N = 0\) is not required to be bounded uniformly over all \(W\)).

Example 2 Let \(G\) be a linear algebraic group, and let \(r_V: G \to \mathrm{GL}_V\) be a representation of \(G\) on a vector space \(V\). For every \(g \in G(k)\), the operator \(r_V(g) \in \mathrm{GL}_V(k) = \operatorname{Aut}_k(V)\) is locally finite.

Corollary 1 Let \(\alpha: V \to V\) be a locally finite operator on a vector space over a perfect field \(k\). Then there exist unique locally finite operators \(\alpha_s, \alpha_u \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(V)\) such that:

  1. \(\alpha = \alpha_s \alpha_u = \alpha_u \alpha_s\);

  2. \(\alpha_s\) is semisimple, and \(\alpha_u\) is locally unipotent.

Theorem 2  

  1. Let \(G\) be a linear algebraic group over a perfect field \(k\). For each \(g \in G(k)\), there exist unique elements \(g_s, g_u \in G(k)\) such that:

    • \(g = g_s g_u = g_u g_s\) (called the Jordan decomposition of \(g\));

    • for every representation \((V, r_V) \in \mathbf{Rep}_k G\), \[ r_V(g_s) = r_V(g)_s \quad \text{and} \quad r_V(g_u) = r_V(g)_u. \]

  2. An element \(g \in G(k)\) is unipotent if \(g_s = 1\), and semisimple if \(g_u = 1\). Equivalently, \(g\) is unipotent (resp. semisimple) if and only if for every representation \((V, r_V) \in \mathbf{Rep}_k G\), the operator \(r_V(g)\) is unipotent (resp. semisimple).

Proof. We use the Tannakian reconstruction theorem: \[ G(k) \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;} \underline{\operatorname{Aut}}^\otimes(\omega)(k), \quad g \longmapsto \big(r_V(g)\big)_{(V, r_V) \in \mathbf{Rep}_k G}. \] Fixing \(g \in G(k)\), for each \((V, r_V) \in \mathbf{Rep}_k G\), the operator \(r_V(g) \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(V)\) admits a unique Jordan decomposition: \[ r_V(g) = r_V(g)_s r_V(g)_u = r_V(g)_u r_V(g)_s. \] It suffices to show that the families \(\big(r_V(g)_s\big)\) and \(\big(r_V(g)_u\big)\) define elements of \(\underline{\operatorname{Aut}}^\otimes(\omega)(k)\):

  1. On the trivial representation \(\mathbf{1} = (k, \text{triv})\), \(r_{\mathbf{1}}(g) = \mathrm{id}_k\), so \(r_{\mathbf{1}}(g)_s = r_{\mathbf{1}}(g)_u = \mathrm{id}_k\).

  2. Compatibility with tensor products: by Proposition 1(3), for any \(V, W \in \mathbf{Rep}_k G\), \[ r_{V \otimes W}(g)_s = \big(r_V(g) \otimes r_W(g)\big)_s = r_V(g)_s \otimes r_W(g)_s, \] and similarly for the unipotent parts.

  3. Functoriality: by Proposition 1(2), for every morphism of representations \(\varphi: W \to V\), \(\varphi\) commutes with \(r_W(g)\) and \(r_V(g)\), hence commutes with their semisimple and unipotent parts.

Thus, \(\big(r_V(g)_s\big), \big(r_V(g)_u\big) \in \underline{\operatorname{Aut}}^\otimes(\omega)(k)\). Tracing back via the Tannakian isomorphism yields unique elements \(g_s, g_u \in G(k)\) such that: \[ r_V(g) = r_V(g)_s r_V(g)_u = r_V(g)_u r_V(g)_s \implies g = g_s g_u = g_u g_s. \]

Corollary 2 Let \(\phi: G \to H\) be a homomorphism of linear algebraic groups over a perfect field \(k\). Then for all \(g \in G(k)\): \[ \phi(g_s) = \phi(g)_s \quad \text{and} \quad \phi(g_u) = \phi(g)_u. \]

Proof. Choose a faithful representation of \(H\), \(r_V: H \hookrightarrow \mathrm{GL}_V\). Then \(r_V \circ \phi\) is a representation of \(G\). By Theorem 2: \[ r_V\big(\phi(g)_s\big) = \big(r_V(\phi(g))\big)_s = \big((r_V \circ \phi)(g)\big)_s = (r_V \circ \phi)(g_s) = r_V\big(\phi(g_s)\big), \] \[ r_V\big(\phi(g)_u\big) = \big(r_V(\phi(g))\big)_u = \big((r_V \circ \phi)(g)\big)_u = (r_V \circ \phi)(g_u) = r_V\big(\phi(g_u)\big). \] Since \(r_V\) is faithful, it is injective on \(k\)-points. Therefore: \[ \phi(g_s) = \phi(g)_s \quad \text{and} \quad \phi(g_u) = \phi(g)_u. \]

Remark 1. An alternative proof of Theorem 2 can be given without Tannakian categories:

  1. By Theorem 5.1.18, choose a faithful representation \(r_V: G \hookrightarrow \mathrm{GL}_V\). In \(\mathrm{GL}_V(k)\), \(r_V(g)\) has a Jordan decomposition \(r_V(g) = r_V(g)_s r_V(g)_u\).

  2. By Chevalley’s theorem (Theorem 5.2.1), there exists a finite-dimensional representation \(W\) constructed from \(V\) (via tensor products, direct sums, duals, and subquotients) and a \(1\)-dimensional subspace \(L \subseteq W\) such that \(G = \operatorname{Stab}_{\mathrm{GL}_V}(L)\).

  3. Let \(\rho: \mathrm{GL}_V \to \mathrm{GL}_W\) be the induced representation. Since \(W\) is constructed from \(V\), by Proposition 1: \[ \rho\big(r_V(g)_s\big) = \rho\big(r_V(g)\big)_s \quad \text{and} \quad \rho\big(r_V(g)_u\big) = \rho\big(r_V(g)\big)_u. \] Since \(r_V(g) \in G(k)\), \(\rho(r_V(g))\) preserves \(L\). As \(L\) is a \(1\)-dimensional invariant subspace, its semisimple and unipotent parts must also preserve \(L\): \[ \rho\big(r_V(g)_s\big)(L) = L \quad \text{and} \quad \rho\big(r_V(g)_u\big)(L) = L. \] Thus, \(r_V(g)_s, r_V(g)_u \in \operatorname{Stab}_{\mathrm{GL}_V}(L)(k) = G(k)\).
