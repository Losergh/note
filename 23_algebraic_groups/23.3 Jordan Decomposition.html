23.3 Jordan Decomposition

Published

October 26, 2026

Motivation (Matrix case): Let \(x \in \mathrm{GL}_n(\bar{k})\). The Jordan normal form of \(x\) is given by: \[ x = s^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} B_{a_1}^{n_1} & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & B_{a_r}^{n_r} \end{pmatrix} s, \] where each \(B_{a_i}^{n_i}\) is a Jordan block of size \(n_i \times n_i\) with eigenvalue \(a_i\): \[ B_{a_i}^{n_i} = \begin{pmatrix} a_i & 1 & & \\ & a_i & \ddots & \\ & & \ddots & 1 \\ & & & a_i \end{pmatrix}. \] Define: \[ x_s := s^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} a_1 I_{n_1} & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & a_r I_{n_r} \end{pmatrix} s \in \mathrm{GL}_n(\bar{k}) \quad (\text{semisimple element}), \] \[ x_u := s^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} a_1^{-1} B_{a_1}^{n_1} & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & a_r^{-1} B_{a_r}^{n_r} \end{pmatrix} s \in \mathrm{GL}_n(\bar{k}) \quad (\text{unipotent element}). \] Then: \[ x = x_s x_u = x_u x_s \] is called the Jordan decomposition of \(x\).

Generalization:

  • Replace the algebraically closed field \(\bar{k}\) with an arbitrary perfect field \(k\).

  • Replace \(\mathrm{GL}_n\) with an arbitrary linear algebraic group \(G\).

Definition 1 Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space over \(k\), and let \(\alpha \in \mathrm{GL}_V(k) = \mathrm{GL}_n(k)\). Let \(P_\alpha(T) := \det(T \cdot \mathrm{id} - \alpha) \in k[T]\) be the characteristic polynomial of \(\alpha\).

  1. The eigenvalues of \(\alpha\) lie in \(k\) if \(P_\alpha(T)\) splits over \(k\): \[ P_\alpha(T) = (T - a_1)^{m_1} \cdots (T - a_r)^{m_r}, \quad a_i \in k. \]

  2. For each eigenvalue \(a \in k\) of \(\alpha\), the primary space (generalized eigenspace) is defined by: \[ V^{(a)} := \{ v \in V \mid (\alpha - a \cdot \mathrm{id})^N v = 0 \text{ for some } N \ge 1 \}. \] Then \(V = \bigoplus_{i=1}^r V^{(a_i)}\), and the usual Jordan decomposition works on each subspace.

  3. An element \(\alpha\) is semisimple if \(\alpha_{\bar{k}} \in \operatorname{End}_{\bar{k}}(V_{\bar{k}})\) is diagonalizable.

  4. An element \(\alpha\) is unipotent if there exists an integer \(N \ge 1\) such that \((\alpha - \mathrm{id})^N = 0\).

Theorem 1 Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space over a perfect field \(k\). Let \(\alpha \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(V) = \mathrm{GL}_V(k)\). Then there exist unique elements \(\alpha_s, \alpha_u \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(V)\) such that:

  1. \(\alpha = \alpha_s \alpha_u = \alpha_u \alpha_s\) (equivalently, \(\alpha = \alpha_s + \alpha_s(\alpha_u - \mathrm{id})\), where \(\alpha_s(\alpha_u - \mathrm{id})\) is nilpotent);

  2. \(\alpha_s\) is semisimple, and \(\alpha_u\) is unipotent.

In this case, \(\alpha_s\) and \(\alpha_u\) are polynomials in \(\alpha\), i.e., \(\alpha_s, \alpha_u \in k[\alpha]\).

Proof. Case 1: All eigenvalues of \(\alpha\) lie in \(k\).

The characteristic polynomial splits over \(k\): \[ P_\alpha(T) = \prod_{i=1}^r (T - a_i)^{m_i}, \quad a_i \in k. \] Then \(V = \bigoplus_{i=1}^r V^{(a_i)}\), where \(V^{(a_i)} = \ker(\alpha - a_i \cdot \mathrm{id})^{m_i}\).

Define \(\alpha_s \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(V)\) by: \[ \alpha_s\big|_{V^{(a_i)}} := a_i \cdot \mathrm{id}_{V^{(a_i)}}. \] Define \(\alpha_u := \alpha \alpha_s^{-1}\). For each \(v_i \in V^{(a_i)}\), we have: \[ (\alpha_u - \mathrm{id})^{m_i}(v_i) = (a_i^{-1} \alpha - \mathrm{id})^{m_i}(v_i) = a_i^{-m_i} (\alpha - a_i \cdot \mathrm{id})^{m_i}(v_i) = 0. \] Hence \(\alpha_u\) is unipotent.

To find a polynomial \(Q(T) \in k[T]\) such that \(Q(\alpha) = \alpha_s\), it suffices to require: \[ \big(Q(\alpha) - a_i \cdot \mathrm{id}\big)\big|_{V^{(a_i)}} = 0 \quad \text{for all } 1 \le i \le r, \] which is guaranteed if: \[ (T - a_i)^{m_i} \mid \big(Q(T) - a_i\big), \quad \text{i.e., } Q(T) \equiv a_i \pmod{(T - a_i)^{m_i}}. \] By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, such a polynomial \(Q(T)\) exists.

Similarly, there exists a polynomial \(\widetilde{Q}(T) \in k[T]\) such that \(\widetilde{Q}(\alpha) = \alpha_s^{-1}\). Then: \[ \alpha_u = \alpha \alpha_s^{-1} = \alpha \widetilde{Q}(\alpha) \] is also a polynomial in \(\alpha\).

Since \(\alpha_s, \alpha_u \in k[\alpha]\), they commute: \(\alpha_s \alpha_u = \alpha_u \alpha_s\). Moreover, any endomorphism \(\beta\) commuting with \(\alpha\) also commutes with \(\alpha_s\) and \(\alpha_u\).

Uniqueness: If \(\alpha = \alpha_s \alpha_u = \beta_s \beta_u\) is another decomposition with commuting factors in \(k[\alpha]\), then: \[ \beta_s^{-1} \alpha_s = \beta_u \alpha_u^{-1} \in k[\alpha]. \] Because \(\beta_s\) and \(\alpha_s\) commute, \(\beta_s^{-1} \alpha_s\) is semisimple. Because \(\beta_u\) and \(\alpha_u\) commute, \(\beta_u \alpha_u^{-1}\) is unipotent (all eigenvalues are \(1\)). The only endomorphism that is simultaneously semisimple and unipotent is the identity. Thus: \[ \beta_s^{-1} \alpha_s = \beta_u \alpha_u^{-1} = \mathrm{id} \implies \alpha_s = \beta_s, \quad \alpha_u = \beta_u. \]

Case 2: General case over a perfect field \(k\).

Since \(k\) is perfect, there exists a finite Galois extension \(k'/k\) such that all eigenvalues of \(\alpha\) lie in \(k'\). Let \(A := \alpha_{k'} \in \operatorname{Aut}_{k'}(V_{k'})\). By Case 1, \(A\) admits a unique Jordan decomposition: \[ A = A_s A_u = A_u A_s. \] For every \(\sigma \in \operatorname{Gal}(k'/k)\), applying \(\sigma\) yields: \[ A = \sigma(A) = \sigma(A_s) \sigma(A_u). \] Observe that:

  1. \(\sigma(A_s)\) is semisimple, and \(\sigma(A_u)\) is unipotent;

  2. \(\sigma(A_s) \sigma(A_u) = \sigma(A_u) \sigma(A_s)\).

By the uniqueness of the Jordan decomposition of \(A\) over \(k'\), we have: \[ \sigma(A_s) = A_s \quad \text{and} \quad \sigma(A_u) = A_u \quad \text{for all } \sigma \in \operatorname{Gal}(k'/k). \] By Galois descent, \(A_s\) and \(A_u\) descend to \(k\), giving \(\alpha_s, \alpha_u \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(V)\) such that \((\alpha_s)_{k'} = A_s\) and \((\alpha_u)_{k'} = A_u\). They satisfy \(\alpha = \alpha_s \alpha_u = \alpha_u \alpha_s\), with \(\alpha_s\) semisimple and \(\alpha_u\) unipotent.

It remains to show that \(\alpha_s, \alpha_u \in k[\alpha]\). We know \(A_s \in k'[A]\). Let \(Q(T) \in k'[T]\) be a polynomial of minimal degree such that \(A_s = Q(A)\). Then \(\deg Q < \deg m_k(A)\), where \(m_k(A)\) is the minimal polynomial of \(A\) over \(k\). Writing \(Q(T) = \sum_{j=0}^r c_j T^j\) with \(c_j \in k'\), we have: \[ A_s = \sigma(A_s) = \sum_{j=0}^r \sigma(c_j) A^j \implies \big(Q - \sigma(Q)\big)(A) = 0. \] If \(\sigma(c_j) \neq c_j\) for some \(j\), this would yield a non-zero polynomial of degree strictly less than \(\deg m_k(A)\) annihilating \(A\), a contradiction. Thus \(\sigma(c_j) = c_j\) for all \(\sigma \in \operatorname{Gal}(k'/k)\) and all \(j\), which implies \(c_j \in k\). Therefore, \(Q(T) \in k[T]\), so \(\alpha_s \in k[\alpha]\). The same argument holds for \(\alpha_u\), and uniqueness follows as in Case 1.

Example 1 Let \(k\) be a nonperfect field of characteristic \(2\), so that the Frobenius map \(x \mapsto x^2\) is not surjective. Choose an element \(a \in k \setminus k^2\). Consider the matrix: \[ \alpha = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ a & 0 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathrm{GL}_2(k). \] Over \(\bar{k}\), its characteristic polynomial is \(T^2 - a = (T - \sqrt{a})^2\). Its Jordan decomposition in \(\mathrm{GL}_2(k(\sqrt{a}))\) is: \[ \alpha = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{a} & 0 \\ 0 & \sqrt{a} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}} \\ \sqrt{a} & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \] where: \[ \alpha_s = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{a} & 0 \\ 0 & \sqrt{a} \end{pmatrix} \quad (\text{semisimple}), \qquad \alpha_u = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}} \\ \sqrt{a} & 0 \end{pmatrix} \quad (\text{unipotent}). \] Since \(\sqrt{a} \notin k\), the components \(\alpha_s\) and \(\alpha_u\) cannot be defined over \(k\). Therefore, \(\alpha\) has no Jordan decomposition in \(\mathrm{GL}_2(k)\).

Proposition 1  

  1. If \(W \subseteq V\) is an \(\alpha\)-stable subspace, then \(W\) is both \(\alpha_s\)-stable and \(\alpha_u\)-stable. The restriction \(\alpha|_W = \alpha_s|_W \cdot \alpha_u|_W\) is the Jordan decomposition of \(\alpha|_W\). The same statement holds for the quotient space \(V/W\).

  2. If \(\varphi: W \to V\) is a \(k\)-linear map intertwining two operators \(\alpha_W \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(W)\) and \(\alpha_V \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(V)\), i.e., \[ \begin{CD} W @>{\varphi}>> V \\ @V{\alpha_W}VV @VV{\alpha_V}V \\ W @>{\varphi}>> V \end{CD} \] commutes, then \(\varphi\) also intertwines their semisimple and unipotent parts: \[ \begin{CD} W @>{\varphi}>> V \\ @V{(\alpha_W)_s}VV @VV{(\alpha_V)_s}V \\ W @>{\varphi}>> V \end{CD} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \begin{CD} W @>{\varphi}>> V \\ @V{(\alpha_W)_u}VV @VV{(\alpha_V)_u}V \\ W @>{\varphi}>> V \end{CD} \]

  3. If \(\alpha \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(V)\) and \(\beta \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(W)\), then: \[ (\alpha \otimes \beta)_s = \alpha_s \otimes \beta_s \quad \text{and} \quad (\alpha \otimes \beta)_u = \alpha_u \otimes \beta_u, \] which gives the Jordan decomposition: \[ \alpha \otimes \beta = (\alpha_s \otimes \beta_s)(\alpha_u \otimes \beta_u) = (\alpha_u \otimes \beta_u)(\alpha_s \otimes \beta_s). \]

Definition 2 Let \(V\) be a vector space over \(k\), and let \(\alpha: V \to V\) be a \(k\)-linear endomorphism.

  1. \(\alpha\) is locally finite if \(V\) is a union of \(\alpha\)-stable finite-dimensional subspaces: \[ V = \bigcup_{\substack{W \subseteq V, \, \dim W < \infty \\ \alpha(W) \subseteq W}} W. \]

  2. If \(\alpha\) is locally finite, then:

    • \(\alpha\) is semisimple if for every finite-dimensional \(\alpha\)-stable subspace \(W \subseteq V\), the restriction \(\alpha|_W\) is semisimple;

    • \(\alpha\) is locally unipotent if for every finite-dimensional \(\alpha\)-stable subspace \(W \subseteq V\), the restriction \(\alpha|_W\) is unipotent (the integer \(N\) such that \((\alpha|_W - \mathrm{id}_W)^N = 0\) is not required to be bounded uniformly over all \(W\)).

Example 2 Let \(G\) be a linear algebraic group, and let \(r_V: G \to \mathrm{GL}_V\) be a representation of \(G\) on a vector space \(V\). For every \(g \in G(k)\), the operator \(r_V(g) \in \mathrm{GL}_V(k) = \operatorname{Aut}_k(V)\) is locally finite.

Corollary 1 Let \(\alpha: V \to V\) be a locally finite operator on a vector space over a perfect field \(k\). Then there exist unique locally finite operators \(\alpha_s, \alpha_u \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(V)\) such that:

  1. \(\alpha = \alpha_s \alpha_u = \alpha_u \alpha_s\);

  2. \(\alpha_s\) is semisimple, and \(\alpha_u\) is locally unipotent.

Theorem 2  

  1. Let \(G\) be a linear algebraic group over a perfect field \(k\). For each \(g \in G(k)\), there exist unique elements \(g_s, g_u \in G(k)\) such that:

    • \(g = g_s g_u = g_u g_s\) (called the Jordan decomposition of \(g\));

    • for every representation \((V, r_V) \in \mathbf{Rep}_k G\), \[ r_V(g_s) = r_V(g)_s \quad \text{and} \quad r_V(g_u) = r_V(g)_u. \]

  2. An element \(g \in G(k)\) is unipotent if \(g_s = 1\), and semisimple if \(g_u = 1\). Equivalently, \(g\) is unipotent (resp. semisimple) if and only if for every representation \((V, r_V) \in \mathbf{Rep}_k G\), the operator \(r_V(g)\) is unipotent (resp. semisimple).

Proof. We use the Tannakian reconstruction theorem: \[ G(k) \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;} \underline{\operatorname{Aut}}^\otimes(\omega)(k), \quad g \longmapsto \big(r_V(g)\big)_{(V, r_V) \in \mathbf{Rep}_k G}. \] Fixing \(g \in G(k)\), for each \((V, r_V) \in \mathbf{Rep}_k G\), the operator \(r_V(g) \in \operatorname{Aut}_k(V)\) admits a unique Jordan decomposition: \[ r_V(g) = r_V(g)_s r_V(g)_u = r_V(g)_u r_V(g)_s. \] It suffices to show that the families \(\big(r_V(g)_s\big)\) and \(\big(r_V(g)_u\big)\) define elements of \(\underline{\operatorname{Aut}}^\otimes(\omega)(k)\):

  1. On the trivial representation \(\mathbf{1} = (k, \text{triv})\), \(r_{\mathbf{1}}(g) = \mathrm{id}_k\), so \(r_{\mathbf{1}}(g)_s = r_{\mathbf{1}}(g)_u = \mathrm{id}_k\).

  2. Compatibility with tensor products: by Proposition 1(3), for any \(V, W \in \mathbf{Rep}_k G\), \[ r_{V \otimes W}(g)_s = \big(r_V(g) \otimes r_W(g)\big)_s = r_V(g)_s \otimes r_W(g)_s, \] and similarly for the unipotent parts.

  3. Functoriality: by Proposition 1(2), for every morphism of representations \(\varphi: W \to V\), \(\varphi\) commutes with \(r_W(g)\) and \(r_V(g)\), hence commutes with their semisimple and unipotent parts.

Thus, \(\big(r_V(g)_s\big), \big(r_V(g)_u\big) \in \underline{\operatorname{Aut}}^\otimes(\omega)(k)\). Tracing back via the Tannakian isomorphism yields unique elements \(g_s, g_u \in G(k)\) such that: \[ r_V(g) = r_V(g)_s r_V(g)_u = r_V(g)_u r_V(g)_s \implies g = g_s g_u = g_u g_s. \]

Corollary 2 Let \(\phi: G \to H\) be a homomorphism of linear algebraic groups over a perfect field \(k\). Then for all \(g \in G(k)\): \[ \phi(g_s) = \phi(g)_s \quad \text{and} \quad \phi(g_u) = \phi(g)_u. \]

Proof. Choose a faithful representation of \(H\), \(r_V: H \hookrightarrow \mathrm{GL}_V\). Then \(r_V \circ \phi\) is a representation of \(G\). By Theorem 2: \[ r_V\big(\phi(g)_s\big) = \big(r_V(\phi(g))\big)_s = \big((r_V \circ \phi)(g)\big)_s = (r_V \circ \phi)(g_s) = r_V\big(\phi(g_s)\big), \] \[ r_V\big(\phi(g)_u\big) = \big(r_V(\phi(g))\big)_u = \big((r_V \circ \phi)(g)\big)_u = (r_V \circ \phi)(g_u) = r_V\big(\phi(g_u)\big). \] Since \(r_V\) is faithful, it is injective on \(k\)-points. Therefore: \[ \phi(g_s) = \phi(g)_s \quad \text{and} \quad \phi(g_u) = \phi(g)_u. \]

Remark 1. An alternative proof of Theorem 2 can be given without Tannakian categories:

  1. By Theorem 5.1.18, choose a faithful representation \(r_V: G \hookrightarrow \mathrm{GL}_V\). In \(\mathrm{GL}_V(k)\), \(r_V(g)\) has a Jordan decomposition \(r_V(g) = r_V(g)_s r_V(g)_u\).

  2. By Chevalley’s theorem (Theorem 5.2.1), there exists a finite-dimensional representation \(W\) constructed from \(V\) (via tensor products, direct sums, duals, and subquotients) and a \(1\)-dimensional subspace \(L \subseteq W\) such that \(G = \operatorname{Stab}_{\mathrm{GL}_V}(L)\).

  3. Let \(\rho: \mathrm{GL}_V \to \mathrm{GL}_W\) be the induced representation. Since \(W\) is constructed from \(V\), by Proposition 1: \[ \rho\big(r_V(g)_s\big) = \rho\big(r_V(g)\big)_s \quad \text{and} \quad \rho\big(r_V(g)_u\big) = \rho\big(r_V(g)\big)_u. \] Since \(r_V(g) \in G(k)\), \(\rho(r_V(g))\) preserves \(L\). As \(L\) is a \(1\)-dimensional invariant subspace, its semisimple and unipotent parts must also preserve \(L\): \[ \rho\big(r_V(g)_s\big)(L) = L \quad \text{and} \quad \rho\big(r_V(g)_u\big)(L) = L. \] Thus, \(r_V(g)_s, r_V(g)_u \in \operatorname{Stab}_{\mathrm{GL}_V}(L)(k) = G(k)\).

Definition 3 (Schematically Dense Subsets (Def 5.3.10)) Let \(X\) be an algebraic scheme over a field \(k\).

  1. A subset \(S \subseteq X(k)\) is schematically dense in \(X\) if the only closed subscheme \(Z \subseteq X\) containing \(S\) (i.e., \(S \subseteq Z(k)\)) is \(X\) itself. Equivalently:

    • \(X\) is reduced (\(X = X^{\mathrm{red}}\)) and \(S\) is topologically dense in \(|X|\) (\(\overline{S} = |X|\));
    • The canonical evaluation morphism \[ \mathcal{O}_X \longrightarrow \prod_{s \in S} i_{s*} k, \quad f \longmapsto \big(f(s)\big)_{s \in S} \] is injective.

  2. A subset \(S\) is schematically dense in \(X\) if and only if it is locally schematically dense (i.e., for any open affine cover \(\{U_i\}\) of \(X\), \(S \cap U_i(k)\) is schematically dense in \(U_i\)).

  3. Schematic density is stable under base change of the base field (by taking the inverse image). In particular, if \(S \subseteq X(k)\) is schematically dense in \(X\), then \(X\) is geometrically reduced.

  4. For a field extension \(k'/k\), a subset \(S \subseteq X(k')\) is schematically dense in \(X\) if the only closed subscheme \(Z \subseteq X\) such that \(S \subseteq Z(k')\) is \(X\) itself.

Proposition 2 (Geometric Reducedness Criterion (Prop 5.3.11)) An algebraic scheme \(X\) over \(k\) is geometrically reduced if and only if \(X(k^s)\) is schematically dense in \(X\), where \(k^s\) denotes the separable closure of \(k\).

Proof. \((\implies)\) Since \(X\) is geometrically reduced, the smooth locus \(X^{\mathrm{sm}}\) is a dense open subscheme of \(X\). Thus it suffices to show that \(X^{\mathrm{sm}}(k^s)\) is dense in \(X^{\mathrm{sm}}\), so we may assume without loss of generality that \(X\) is smooth over \(k\).

Then the base change \(X_{k^s}\) is smooth over \(k^s\). For each point \(x \in X_{k^s}\), there exists an étale neighborhood \(U \xrightarrow{f} \mathbb{A}^d_{k^s}\). Since \(k^s\) is separably closed, every non-empty étale \(k^s\)-scheme has \(k^s\)-rational points, which implies that the rational points are topologically dense: \[ \overline{X_{k^s}(k^s)} = |X_{k^s}|. \] Under the projection morphism \(\pi: X_{k^s} \to X\), the image of \(X_{k^s}(k^s)\) is \(X(k^s)\). Hence: \[ \overline{X(k^s)} = \overline{\pi\big(X_{k^s}(k^s)\big)} = \pi\big(\overline{X_{k^s}(k^s)}\big) = \pi(|X_{k^s}|) = |X|. \] Because \(X\) is reduced and \(X(k^s)\) is dense in \(|X|\), \(X(k^s)\) is schematically dense in \(X\).

\((\impliedby)\) If \(X(k^s)\) is schematically dense in \(X\), then by stability under base change (Definition 3(3)), \(X_{k^s}(k^s)\) is dense in \(X_{k^s}\), which implies that \(X_{\bar{k}}(\bar{k})\) is dense in \(X_{\bar{k}}\). A scheme containing a dense set of reduced points is reduced, hence \(X_{\bar{k}}\) is reduced, which means \(X\) is geometrically reduced.

Definition 4 (Frobenius Morphisms (Def 5.3.12)) Let \(k\) be a field of characteristic \(p > 0\).

  1. For a \(k\)-algebra \(R\), the \(p\)-th power map \(\sigma: R \to R\), \(x \mapsto x^p\) is a ring homomorphism (the Frobenius endomorphism), but it is not a \(k\)-algebra homomorphism unless \(k = \mathbb{F}_p\). Geometrically, this induces the absolute Frobenius morphism: \[ \begin{CD} \operatorname{Spec} R @>{\sigma^*}>> \operatorname{Spec} R \\ @VVV @VVV \\ \operatorname{Spec} k @>{\sigma^*}>> \operatorname{Spec} k \end{CD} \]

  2. For a \(k\)-scheme \(X\), the Frobenius twist \(X^{(p)}\) is defined by the fiber product (base change by \(\sigma: k \to k\), \(a \mapsto a^p\)): \[ \begin{CD} X^{(p)} @>>> X \\ @VVV @VVV \\ \operatorname{Spec} k @>{\sigma}>> \operatorname{Spec} k \end{CD} \] By the universal property of fiber products, the absolute Frobenius \(F_{\mathrm{abs}}: X \to X\) and the structure morphism \(X \to \operatorname{Spec} k\) induce a unique morphism of \(k\)-schemes: \[ F_{X/k}: X \longrightarrow X^{(p)}, \] called the relative Frobenius morphism.

  3. In terms of functors of points: for a \(k\)-algebra \(R\), let \({}_\sigma R\) (or \({}_f R\)) denote the \(k\)-algebra with underlying ring \(R\) whose \(k\)-algebra structure is twisted by \(\sigma\) (i.e., \(a \cdot r := \sigma(a) r = a^p r\)). Then the \(p\)-th power map \(R \to {}_\sigma R\), \(x \mapsto x^p\) is a homomorphism of \(k\)-algebras, and: \[ X^{(p)}(R) \cong X({}_\sigma R). \]

Proposition 3 (Properties of Relative Frobenius (Prop 5.3.13))  

  1. Functoriality: For any morphism of \(k\)-schemes \(\varphi: X \to Y\), the relative Frobenius is functorial; that is, the following diagram commutes: \[ \begin{CD} X @>{\varphi}>> Y \\ @V{F_{X/k}}VV @VV{F_{Y/k}}V \\ X^{(p)} @>{\varphi^{(p)}}>> Y^{(p)} \end{CD} \]

  2. Compatibility with products: \[ F_{(X \times_k Y)/k} = F_{X/k} \times F_{Y/k}. \]

  3. Compatibility with base change: For any field extension \(k'/k\), \[ (F_{X/k})_{k'} = F_{X_{k'}/k'}. \]

  4. Group scheme structure: If \(G\) is an algebraic group over \(k\), then the functor \[ G^{(p)}: \mathbf{Alg}_k \longrightarrow \mathbf{Grp}, \quad R \longmapsto G^{(p)}(R) = G({}_\sigma R) \] defines an algebraic group over \(k\), and the relative Frobenius morphism \(F_G: G \to G^{(p)}\) is a homomorphism of algebraic groups.

Proof. Part (4) follows from applying (1) and (2) to the multiplication map \(m: G \times G \to G\). Specifically, the diagram: \[ \begin{CD} G \times G @>{m}>> G \\ @V{F_G \times F_G}VV @VV{F_G}V \\ G^{(p)} \times G^{(p)} @>{m^{(p)}}>> G^{(p)} \end{CD} \] commutes, showing that \(F_G\) preserves multiplication, hence is a homomorphism of algebraic groups.

Proposition 4 (Relative Frobenius of Smooth Connected Groups (Prop 5.3.14)) If \(G\) is a smooth and connected (i.e., geometrically integral) algebraic group over \(k\), then \(G^{(p)}\) is also smooth and connected. Moreover, the relative Frobenius morphism \(F_G: G \to G^{(p)}\) is fppf (faithfully flat of finite presentation).

Proof. By descent of smoothness and fppf descent of fppf morphisms, we may assume without loss of generality that \(k = \bar{k}\).

When \(k = \bar{k}\) (hence perfect), \(\sigma: k \xrightarrow{\sim} k\) is an isomorphism of fields, so \(G^{(p)} \cong G\) as schemes; in particular, \(G^{(p)}\) is smooth and connected.

On coordinate rings, the pullback map \((F_G)^*: \mathcal{O}(G^{(p)}) \hookrightarrow \mathcal{O}(G)\) is the Frobenius map (\(a \mapsto a^p\)), which is integral (every element \(a\) satisfies \(T^p - a^p = 0\)). By generic flatness and the homogeneity of algebraic groups, since \(G^{(p)}\) is regular/reduced, \(F_G\) is flat everywhere. Because it is dominant and integral, it is surjective on points. Hence \(F_G: G \to G^{(p)}\) is fppf.

Corollary 3 (Iterated Frobenius (Corollary 5.3.15)) For each \(n \ge 1\), define the \(n\)-th Frobenius twist inductively by \(G^{(p^n)} := (G^{(p^{n-1})})^{(p)}\) with \(G^{(p^0)} := G\). Then the \(n\)-th iterate of relative Frobenius: \[ F_G^n: G \xrightarrow{F_G} G^{(p)} \xrightarrow{F_{G^{(p)}}} \cdots \xrightarrow{F_{G^{(p^{n-1})}}} G^{(p^n)} \] is a homomorphism of algebraic groups. If \(G\) is smooth and connected, then \(G^{(p^n)}\) is smooth and connected, and \(F_G^n\) is fppf.

Proposition 5 (Hopf Subalgebra \(\mathcal{O}(G)^{p^n}\) (Prop 5.3.16)) Let \(G\) be a linear algebraic group over a perfect field \(k\) of characteristic \(p > 0\). For \(n \ge 1\), let \(\mathcal{O}(G)^{p^n} := \operatorname{Im}(f^n)\), where \(f^n: \mathcal{O}(G) \to \mathcal{O}(G)\) is the \(p^n\)-th power map \(a \mapsto a^{p^n}\). Then \(\mathcal{O}(G)^{p^n}\) is a Hopf subalgebra of \(\mathcal{O}(G)\), which is geometrically reduced for all \(n \gg 0\).

Proof. Let \(A := \mathcal{O}(G)\) be the coordinate Hopf algebra of \(G\). The comorphism of the \(n\)-th relative Frobenius is: \[ (F_G^n)^*: A \otimes_{k, \sigma^n} k \longrightarrow A, \quad a \otimes b \longmapsto a^{p^n} b. \] Since \(k\) is perfect, every \(b \in k\) has a unique \(p^n\)-th root \(b^{1/p^n} \in k\), so \(a^{p^n} b = (a b^{1/p^n})^{p^n}\). Thus: \[ \operatorname{Im}\big((F_G^n)^*\big) = A^{p^n} = \mathcal{O}(G)^{p^n}. \] Since \(F_G^n: G \to G^{(p^n)}\) is a homomorphism of algebraic groups, the image of its comorphism is a Hopf subalgebra of \(A\).

Because \(A\) is a Noetherian ring, its nilradical \(\operatorname{nil}(A)\) is finitely generated, hence nilpotent: there exists an integer \(n_0 \ge 1\) such that \(\operatorname{nil}(A)^{p^{n_0}} = 0\). Taking \(n \gg 0\) such that \(p^n > n_0\), any nilpotent element in \(A\) has its \(p^n\)-th power equal to \(0\). Therefore, \(\mathcal{O}(G)^{p^n}\) has no non-zero nilpotent elements, so it is reduced. Because \(k\) is perfect, reduced implies geometrically reduced (reduced over \(k = k^{p^n} \iff\) reduced over \(\bar{k}\)).

Corollary 4 (Factorization via Smooth Algebraic Groups (Corollary 5.3.17)) Let \(G\) be a linear algebraic group over a perfect field \(k\) of characteristic \(p > 0\). Consider the \(n\)-th relative Frobenius \(F_G^n: G \to G^{(p^n)}\), and let \(I_n := \operatorname{Spec}\big(\mathcal{O}(G)^{p^n}\big) = \operatorname{Spec}\big(\operatorname{Im}((F_G^n)^*)\big)\) be its scheme-theoretic image: \[ \begin{CD} G @>{F_G^n}>> G^{(p^n)} \\ @V{\text{fppf}}VV @AAA \\ I_n @= I_n \end{CD} \]

  1. If \(G\) is smooth and connected, then \(F_G^n\) is fppf, hence \(I_n = G^{(p^n)}\), which is smooth and connected.

  2. In general, for all \(n \gg 0\), \(I_n\) is a smooth linear algebraic group over \(k\), and the canonical morphism \(G \to I_n\) is fppf.

Lemma 1 (Smoothness Criterion via Nilradical (Lemma 5.3.18)) Let \(G\) be a linear algebraic group over an algebraically closed field \(k\). If \(\operatorname{nil}(\mathcal{O}(G)) \subseteq \mathfrak{m}_e^2\), where \(\mathfrak{m}_e\) is the maximal ideal at the identity element \(e \in G\), then \(G\) is smooth.

Proof. An algebraic group \(G\) is smooth if and only if \(\dim T_e G = \dim G\). Let \(G^{\mathrm{red}}\) be the associated reduced algebraic group. Over an algebraically closed field \(k\), \(G^{\mathrm{red}}\) is a smooth algebraic group, so: \[ \dim T_e G^{\mathrm{red}} = \dim G^{\mathrm{red}} = \dim G. \] Thus it suffices to show that \(\dim T_e G = \dim T_e G^{\mathrm{red}}\).

The maximal ideal of the local ring of \(G^{\mathrm{red}}\) at \(e\) is \(\mathfrak{m}_e^{\mathrm{red}} = \mathfrak{m}_e / \operatorname{nil}(\mathcal{O}(G))\). The canonical quotient morphism on Zariski cotangent spaces: \[ \mathfrak{m}_e / \mathfrak{m}_e^2 \longrightarrow \mathfrak{m}_e^{\mathrm{red}} / (\mathfrak{m}_e^{\mathrm{red}})^2 \cong \mathfrak{m}_e / \big(\mathfrak{m}_e^2 + \operatorname{nil}(\mathcal{O}(G))\big) \] is an isomorphism because \(\operatorname{nil}(\mathcal{O}(G)) \subseteq \mathfrak{m}_e^2\) implies \(\mathfrak{m}_e^2 + \operatorname{nil}(\mathcal{O}(G)) = \mathfrak{m}_e^2\). Dualizing gives an isomorphism on tangent spaces \(T_e G^{\mathrm{red}} \xrightarrow{\sim} T_e G\), so: \[ \dim T_e G = \dim T_e G^{\mathrm{red}} = \dim G. \] Hence \(G\) is smooth at \(e\), and by group translations, \(G\) is smooth everywhere.

Theorem 3 (Cartier’s Theorem (Theorem 5.3.19)) Every linear algebraic group (and indeed every group scheme of finite type) over a field \(k\) of characteristic zero is smooth.

Proof. By fppf descent of smoothness, we may assume without loss of generality that \(k = \bar{k}\).

Write \(G = \operatorname{Spec} A\), where \(A = \mathcal{O}(G)\) is the coordinate Hopf algebra of \(G\), and let \(\mathfrak{p} := \mathfrak{m}_e = \ker(\epsilon: A \to k)\) be the augmentation ideal corresponding to the identity element \(e \in G\). By Lemma 1, it suffices to show that every nilpotent element \(a \in A\) satisfies \(a \in \mathfrak{p}^2\).

Since \(A/\mathfrak{p} \cong k\) has no non-zero nilpotents, \(a \in \mathfrak{p}\). If the image of \(a\) in the localization \(A_{\mathfrak{p}}\) is zero, then its image in \(A_{\mathfrak{p}}/(\mathfrak{p}A_{\mathfrak{p}})^2 \cong A/\mathfrak{p}^2\) is zero, which implies \(a \in \mathfrak{p}^2\), and there is nothing to prove.

Thus, we may assume that the image of \(a\) in \(A_{\mathfrak{p}}\) is non-zero. Let \(n \ge 2\) be an integer such that: \[ a^n = 0 \text{ in } A_{\mathfrak{p}}, \quad \text{but} \quad a^{n-1} \neq 0 \text{ in } A_{\mathfrak{p}}. \] Since \(a^n = 0\) in \(A_{\mathfrak{p}}\), there exists an element \(s \in A \setminus \mathfrak{p}\) such that \(s a^n = 0\) in \(A\). Replacing \(a\) with \(sa\) (note that \(sa \equiv s(e) a \pmod{\mathfrak{p}^2}\) with \(s(e) \in k^\times\), so \(a \in \mathfrak{p}^2 \iff sa \in \mathfrak{p}^2\), and \((sa)^{n-1} = s^{n-1} a^{n-1} \neq 0\) in \(A_{\mathfrak{p}}\)), we may assume: \[ a^n = 0 \text{ in } A, \quad \text{and} \quad a^{n-1} \neq 0 \text{ in } A_{\mathfrak{p}}. \]

Claim: \(m^*(a) - a \otimes 1 - 1 \otimes a \in \mathfrak{p} \otimes \mathfrak{p}\), where \(m^*: A \to A \otimes_k A\) is the comultiplication map.

Proof of Claim: The counit axioms state that: \[ (\epsilon \otimes \mathrm{id}) \circ m^* = \mathrm{id} \quad \text{and} \quad (\mathrm{id} \otimes \epsilon) \circ m^* = \mathrm{id}. \] Since \(\mathfrak{p} = \ker \epsilon\), we have: \[ m^*(a) \equiv 1 \otimes a \pmod{\mathfrak{p} \otimes A} \quad \text{and} \quad m^*(a) \equiv a \otimes 1 \pmod{A \otimes \mathfrak{p}}. \] Under the decomposition \(A = k \cdot 1 \oplus \mathfrak{p}\), \[ A \otimes_k A = (k \cdot 1 \otimes 1) \oplus (k \cdot 1 \otimes \mathfrak{p}) \oplus (\mathfrak{p} \otimes k \cdot 1) \oplus (\mathfrak{p} \otimes \mathfrak{p}). \] Comparing with the congruences above yields: \[ m^*(a) = a \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes a + y \quad \text{for some } y \in \mathfrak{p} \otimes_k \mathfrak{p}. \] This establishes the Claim.

Now, because \(m^*\) is a \(k\)-algebra homomorphism and \(a^n = 0\) in \(A\): \[ 0 = m^*(a^n) = \big(m^*(a)\big)^n = (a \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes a + y)^n. \] Expanding this product modulo \(A \otimes \mathfrak{p}^2\), note that any term containing \((1 \otimes a + y)^k\) with \(k \ge 2\) lies in \(A \otimes \mathfrak{p}^2\) because \(1 \otimes a + y \in A \otimes \mathfrak{p}\). Therefore: \[ 0 \equiv a^n \otimes 1 + n (a^{n-1} \otimes 1)(1 \otimes a + y) \pmod{A \otimes \mathfrak{p}^2}. \] Since \(a^n = 0\) in \(A\), this reduces to: \[ n a^{n-1} \otimes a + n (a^{n-1} \otimes 1) y \in A \otimes \mathfrak{p}^2. \] Because \(y \in \mathfrak{p} \otimes \mathfrak{p}\), we have \((a^{n-1} \otimes 1) y \in a^{n-1}\mathfrak{p} \otimes \mathfrak{p}\). Thus: \[ n a^{n-1} \otimes a \in a^{n-1}\mathfrak{p} \otimes \mathfrak{p} + A \otimes \mathfrak{p}^2. \] Passing to the quotient \(A \otimes (\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{p}^2)\), and denoting the image of \(a\) by \(\bar{a} \in \mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{p}^2\), we obtain: \[ n a^{n-1} \otimes \bar{a} \in a^{n-1}\mathfrak{p} \otimes (\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{p}^2) \quad \text{in } A \otimes_k (\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{p}^2). \] If \(\bar{a} \neq 0\) in \(\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{p}^2\), then \(\bar{a}\) can be extended to a basis of \(\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{p}^2\), which forces: \[ n a^{n-1} \in a^{n-1}\mathfrak{p}. \] Since \(\operatorname{char}(k) = 0\), the integer \(n\) is invertible in \(k\), so: \[ a^{n-1} \in a^{n-1}\mathfrak{p}. \] Thus there exists an element \(x \in \mathfrak{p}\) such that \(a^{n-1} = a^{n-1} x\), which implies: \[ a^{n-1}(1 - x) = 0. \] Because \(x \in \mathfrak{p} = \mathfrak{m}_e\), the element \(1 - x\) is a unit in the local ring \(A_{\mathfrak{p}}\). Multiplying by its inverse in \(A_{\mathfrak{p}}\) yields: \[ a^{n-1} = 0 \quad \text{in } A_{\mathfrak{p}}, \] which directly contradicts our choice of \(n\) (that \(a^{n-1} \neq 0\) in \(A_{\mathfrak{p}}\)).

Therefore, we must have \(\bar{a} = 0\) in \(\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{p}^2\), which means \(a \in \mathfrak{p}^2\). By Lemma 1, \(G\) is smooth.
