22.10 Connectedness II

Secant and Tangent Varieties, Zak’s Theorems on Tangencies, Ramification, and Linear Normality

Published

October 26, 2026

Definition 1 (Secant and Tangent Varieties) Let \(X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r\) be a smooth irreducible subvariety of dimension \(n\). We define the secant variety \(\operatorname{Sec}(X)\) and the tangent variety \(\operatorname{Tan}(X)\) via the secant and tangent bundles:

  1. Tangent variety via Grassmannian incidence: Let \(G = \operatorname{Gr}(n, \mathbb{P}^r) \cong \operatorname{Gr}(n+1, r+1)\) be the Grassmannian of \(n\)-planes in \(\mathbb{P}^r\), and let: \[ \mathcal{I} := \big\{ (x, L) \in \mathbb{P}^r \times G \;\big|\; x \in L \big\} \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r \times G \] be the universal incidence family over \(G\).

    The Euler sequence on \(\mathbb{P}^r\) restricted to \(X\) gives: \[ 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal{O}_X \longrightarrow \mathcal{O}_X(1)^{\oplus (r+1)} \longrightarrow T_{\mathbb{P}^r}|_X \longrightarrow 0. \] The subbundle \(T_X(-1) \subseteq T_{\mathbb{P}^r}|_X(-1)\) lifts to a subbundle \(\widehat{T}_X \subseteq \mathcal{O}_X^{\oplus (r+1)}\) of rank \(n+1\), defining the Gauss map \(f: X \to G\), \(x \mapsto \mathbb{T}_x\). Pulling back the universal family: \[ \begin{CD} \mathcal{I} @>{\pi_2}>> \mathbb{P}^r \\ @V{\pi_1}VV \\ G \end{CD} \] the tangent variety is the closed subvariety defined by: \[ \operatorname{Tan}(X) := \pi_2\big(\pi_1^{-1}(f(X))\big) \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r. \]

  2. Secant variety via the Hilbert scheme of two points: Let \(X^{[2]} := \operatorname{Hilb}_2(X)\) be the Hilbert scheme of length-\(2\) zero-dimensional subschemes of \(X\), and let \(\mathcal{Z} \subseteq X \times X^{[2]} \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r \times X^{[2]}\) be the universal subscheme. The projection \(\pi_2: \mathcal{Z} \to X^{[2]}\) is finite flat of degree \(2\).

    The evaluation map on global sections gives a surjection: \[ \mathcal{O}_{X^{[2]}}^{\oplus (r+1)} \twoheadrightarrow \mathcal{E} := {\pi_2}_* \mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Z}}(1), \] where \(\mathcal{E}\) is locally free of rank \(2\) on \(X^{[2]}\). This induces a morphism: \[ f: X^{[2]} \longrightarrow \operatorname{Gr}(1, \mathbb{P}^r) = \operatorname{Gr}(2, r+1), \] mapping each length-\(2\) subscheme to the unique line it spans in \(\mathbb{P}^r\). Let \(\mathcal{U} = \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}) \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r \times X^{[2]}\) be the family of lines over \(X^{[2]}\) with projections \(p_1: \mathcal{U} \to X^{[2]}\) and \(p_2: \mathcal{U} \to \mathbb{P}^r\). The secant variety is the irreducible subvariety: \[ \operatorname{Sec}(X) := p_2(\mathcal{U}) = p_2\big(p_1^{-1}(X^{[2]})\big) \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r. \] Under the Hilbert–Chow morphism \(\varphi: X^{[2]} \to X^{(2)} = (X \times X) / S_2\), let \(\Delta^{[2]} := \varphi^{-1}(\Delta)\) denote the exceptional divisor parameterizing non-reduced subschemes. Then the tangent variety can also be realized as: \[ \operatorname{Tan}(X) = p_2\big(p_1^{-1}(\Delta^{[2]})\big) \subseteq \operatorname{Sec}(X), \] which is an irreducible closed subvariety of \(\mathbb{P}^r\).

Proposition 1 (Dimension of \(\operatorname{Sec}(X)\) and \(\operatorname{Tan}(X)\)) Let \(X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r\) be a smooth irreducible subvariety of dimension \(n\). Then either: \[ \dim \operatorname{Sec}(X) = 2n + 1 \quad \text{and} \quad \dim \operatorname{Tan}(X) = 2n, \] or: \[ \operatorname{Sec}(X) = \operatorname{Tan}(X). \]

Proof. Assume \(\operatorname{Tan}(X) \neq \operatorname{Sec}(X)\). It suffices to prove that \(\dim \operatorname{Tan}(X) \ge 2n\). (Indeed, if \(\dim \operatorname{Tan}(X) = 2n\), then \(2n + 1 \le \dim \operatorname{Sec}(X) \le \dim X^{[2]} + 1 = 2n + 1\), forcing \(\dim \operatorname{Sec}(X) = 2n + 1\)).

Suppose for contradiction that \(\dim \operatorname{Tan}(X) < 2n\). Since \(\dim \operatorname{Sec}(X) > \dim \operatorname{Tan}(X)\), we can choose a general linear subspace \(L \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r\) of codimension \(\le 2n\) such that: \[ L \cap \operatorname{Tan}(X) = \varnothing \quad \text{but} \quad L \cap \operatorname{Sec}(X) \neq \varnothing. \] In particular, \(L \cap X = \varnothing\). Projection away from the center \(L\) yields a morphism: \[ \varphi: X \longrightarrow \mathbb{P}^{r'}, \quad \text{where } r' \le \operatorname{codim}(L) - 1 < 2n. \] Because \(L \cap \operatorname{Tan}(X) = \varnothing\), no tangent space of \(X\) intersects \(L\), which means the differential \(d\varphi_x\) is injective everywhere; hence \(\varphi\) is unramified. On the other hand, because \(L \cap \operatorname{Sec}(X) \neq \varnothing\), there exists a secant line of \(X\) meeting \(L\), so \(\varphi\) is not injective on closed points.

However, since \(X\) is complete and \(2n > r'\), the Ramification Theorem implies that any unramified morphism \(\varphi: X \to \mathbb{P}^{r'}\) must be a closed embedding, hence injective on points. This contradiction shows that \(\dim \operatorname{Tan}(X) \ge 2n\).

Definition 2 (Local Degree and Ramification Loci) Let \(f: X \to Y\) be a finite surjective morphism of irreducible varieties of dimension \(n\) over \(\mathbb{C}\).

  1. Ramification and Branch Loci: If \(Y\) is smooth, the relative cotangent sheaf \(\Omega^1_{X/Y}\) has generic stalk \((\Omega^1_{X/Y})_\eta = \Omega^1_{k(X)/k(Y)} = 0\) (since \(\mathbb{C}\) is of characteristic zero). The open set: \[ U := X \setminus \operatorname{Supp}(\Omega^1_{X/Y}) \] is the unramified locus. The closed subset \(R := X \setminus U\) is the ramification locus, and \(B(f) := f(R) \subseteq Y\) is the branch locus.

  2. Étaleness over the Unbranched Locus: Over the complement \(Y \setminus B(f)\), the restriction \(f^{-1}(Y \setminus B(f)) \to Y \setminus B(f)\) is étale. Indeed, for any \(y \in Y \setminus B(f)\) and \(x \in f^{-1}(y)\): \[ \dim X \le \dim T_x X \le \dim T_y Y = \dim Y = \dim X, \] which implies \(\dim T_x X = \dim X\), so \(X\) is smooth at \(x\). By Miracle Flatness, a surjective morphism from a Cohen–Macaulay variety to a regular variety with equidimensional fibers is flat; hence \(f\) is flat and unramified, thus étale. (If \(Y\) is not smooth, this may fail: consider the normalization of a nodal curve \(\mathbb{A}^1 \to C\)).

  3. Local Degree: Analytically, for every \(y \in Y\), there exists an open neighborhood \(U(y) \subseteq Y\) such that: \[ f^{-1}(U(y)) = \coprod_{x \in f^{-1}(y)} V(x), \] where each \(V(x) \subseteq X\) is a disjoint open neighborhood of \(x\), and each restriction \(f|_{V(x)}: V(x) \to U(y)\) is finite (quasi-finite and proper). The local degree \(e_f(x)\) is defined by: \[ e_f(x) := \deg\big(V(x) \to U(y)\big). \] Since \(B(f)\) is a proper closed subvariety of \(Y\), \(U(y) \setminus B(f)\) is dense in \(U(y)\). Taking a sequence \(y_k \to y\) in \(U(y) \setminus B(f)\), \(f|_{V(x)}\) is a topological covering of degree \(e_f(x)\) over \(y_k\), so: \[ e_f(x) = \lim_{k \to \infty} \#\big(f|_{V(x)}^{-1}(y_k)\big), \quad \text{and} \quad \deg f = \sum_{x \in f^{-1}(y)} e_f(x). \] The function \(x \mapsto e_f(x)\) is upper semicontinuous in the analytic topology.

  4. Higher Ramification Loci: For any integer \(l \ge 1\), the \(l\)-th higher ramification locus is: \[ R_l(f) := \big\{ x \in X \;\big|\; e_f(x) > l \big\}. \] In particular, \(R_1(f) = R(f)\). By Serre’s GAGA, every analytic closed subset in a projective variety is Zariski closed, so \(R_l(f)\) is a Zariski closed subvariety of \(X\).

Lemma 1 (Additivity of Local Degree) Let \(f: X \to Y\) be a finite surjective morphism of complex varieties. Let \(\{x_k\}\) and \(\{z_k\}\) be two sequences of points in \(X\) such that \(x_k \neq z_k\), \(f(x_k) = f(z_k)\), and: \[ \lim_{k \to \infty} x_k = \lim_{k \to \infty} z_k = x^* \in X. \] Then: \[ e_f(x^*) \ge \limsup_{k \to \infty} \big(e_f(x_k) + e_f(z_k)\big). \]

Proof. Work in the analytic topology. Choose disjoint neighborhoods \(V(x^*)\) and \(U(f(x^*))\) such that \(f|_{V(x^*)}: V(x^*) \to U(f(x^*))\) is finite of degree \(e_f(x^*)\). For \(k \gg 0\), both \(x_k, z_k \in V(x^*)\), and \(y_k := f(x_k) = f(z_k) \in U(f(x^*))\).

For any point \(y \in U(f(x^*))\), the degree is the sum of local degrees over the fiber in \(V(x^*)\): \[ e_f(x^*) = \sum_{w \in V(x^*) \cap f^{-1}(y_k)} e_f(w) \ge e_f(x_k) + e_f(z_k). \] Since \(e_f\) takes values in \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\), taking the limit superior yields \(e_f(x^*) \ge \limsup_{k \to \infty} (e_f(x_k) + e_f(z_k))\).

Theorem 1 (Ramification of Coverings of Projective Space) Let \(X\) be an irreducible projective variety of dimension \(n\), and let \(f: X \to \mathbb{P}^n\) be a finite branched covering of degree \(d\).

  1. There exists a point \(x \in X\) such that: \[ e_f(x) \ge \min\{d, \, n + 1\}. \]
  2. More generally, for each \(1 \le l \le \min\{d - 1, \, n\}\), the higher ramification locus satisfies: \[ \operatorname{codim}_X R_l(f) \le l. \] In particular, for \(l = \min\{d - 1, \, n\}\), the locus \(R_l(f) = \{ x \in X \mid e_f(x) \ge \min\{d, n+1\} \}\) has codimension \(\le n\), hence is non-empty.

Proof. If \(d = 1\), the map is birational and finite, hence an isomorphism since \(\mathbb{P}^n\) is normal; the claim is trivial. Assume \(d \ge 2\). We proceed by induction on \(n\):

  • Base case \(n = 1\): Here \(l = 1\), and we must show \(R_1(f) \neq \varnothing\). If \(R_1(f) = \varnothing\), then \(f: X \to \mathbb{P}^1\) is a finite étale cover of degree \(d \ge 2\). But \(\mathbb{P}^1\) is simply connected (\(\pi_1^{\text{ét}}(\mathbb{P}^1) = \{e\}\)), so it admits no non-trivial connected étale covers, a contradiction.

  • Inductive step \(n \ge 2\): Let \(L \subseteq \mathbb{P}^n\) be a general hyperplane such that \(X' := f^{-1}(L)\) is irreducible (by Bertini’s Theorem). Let \(f' := f|_{X'}: X' \to L \cong \mathbb{P}^{n-1}\), which is a finite covering of degree \(d\).

    For a general hyperplane \(L\), \(L\) meets the unbranched locus \(V \subseteq \mathbb{P}^n\), and for any \(x \in f^{-1}(L)\), we have \(e_{f'}(x) = e_f(x)\). Thus \(R_l(f') = R_l(f) \cap X'\). By induction, for all \(l \le \min\{d - 1, \, n - 1\}\): \[ \operatorname{codim}_{X'} R_l(f') \le l \implies \operatorname{codim}_X R_l(f) \le l. \]

  • The case \(l = n\) (when \(d \ge n + 1\)): It remains to prove that \(R_n(f) \neq \varnothing\), i.e., there exists \(s^* \in X\) with \(e_f(s^*) \ge n + 1\). By the codimension bound for \(l = n - 1\), we have \(\operatorname{codim}_X R_{n-1}(f) \le n - 1\), so: \[ \dim R_{n-1}(f) \ge n - (n - 1) = 1. \] Let \(S\) be an irreducible component of \(R_{n-1}(f)\) of dimension \(\ge 1\). Consider the product morphism: \[ F := f \times f|_S: X \times S \longrightarrow \mathbb{P}^n \times \mathbb{P}^n. \] The space \(X \times S\) is irreducible of dimension \(\dim(X \times S) = n + \dim S \ge n + 1 > n\). Since \(F\) is finite, \(\dim F(X \times S) \ge n + 1 > n\). By the Fulton–Hansen Connectedness Theorem, the preimage: \[ F^{-1}(\Delta_{\mathbb{P}^n}) = X \times_{\mathbb{P}^n} S = \big\{ (x, s) \in X \times S \;\big|\; f(x) = f(s) \big\} \] is connected.

    Notice that \(\Delta_S := \{ (s, s) \mid s \in S \} \subseteq F^{-1}(\Delta_{\mathbb{P}^n})\) is an irreducible component of dimension \(\dim S\).

    • If \(\Delta_S = F^{-1}(\Delta_{\mathbb{P}^n})\), then \(f(x) = f(s)\) implies \(x = s\), meaning every point \(s \in S\) has a unique preimage \(\# f^{-1}(f(s)) = 1\). Thus \(e_f(s) = d \ge n + 1\), which gives \(S \subseteq R_n(f)\), so \(R_n(f) \neq \varnothing\).
    • If \(\Delta_S \subsetneq F^{-1}(\Delta_{\mathbb{P}^n})\), by connectedness of \(F^{-1}(\Delta_{\mathbb{P}^n})\) there exists another irreducible component \(T \neq \Delta_S\) meeting \(\Delta_S\): \[ T \cap \Delta_S \neq \varnothing. \] Choose a point \((s^*, s^*) \in T \cap \Delta_S\) and a sequence \((x_k, s_k) \in T \setminus \Delta_S\) converging to \((s^*, s^*)\) with \(x_k \neq s_k\) and \(f(x_k) = f(s_k)\). Then \(\lim x_k = \lim s_k = s^*\). Because \(s_k \in S \subseteq R_{n-1}(f)\), we have \(e_f(s_k) \ge n\), and trivially \(e_f(x_k) \ge 1\). By Lemma 1: \[ e_f(s^*) \ge \limsup_{k \to \infty}\big(e_f(s_k) + e_f(x_k)\big) \ge n + 1. \] Therefore, \(s^* \in R_n(f)\), completing the proof.

Corollary 1 (Geometric Consequences for Branched Coverings) Let \(X\) be an irreducible projective variety of dimension \(n\), and let \(f: X \to \mathbb{P}^n\) be a branched covering of degree \(d\).

  1. If \(d \le n + 1\), there exists a subvariety \(T \subseteq \mathbb{P}^n\) of dimension \(\ge n + 1 - d\) over which \(f\) is bijective.
  2. If \(X\) is normal and \(d \le n\), then the étale fundamental group of \(X\) is trivial: \[ \pi_1^{\text{ét}}(X) = \{e\}. \]

Proof.

  1. For \(l = d - 1 \le n\), Theorem 1 implies that \(\operatorname{codim}_X R_{d-1}(f) \le d - 1\). For any \(x \in R_{d-1}(f)\), we have \(e_f(x) \ge d\), which forces \(f^{-1}(f(x)) = \{x\}\) because \(\sum_{x' \in f^{-1}(f(x))} e_f(x') = d\). Hence \(f\) is bijective over \(T := f(R_{d-1}(f))\), where \(\dim T \ge n - (d - 1) = n + 1 - d\).

  2. Suppose \(p: Y \to X\) is a connected finite étale cover of degree \(m \ge 2\). Because \(X\) is normal and connected, \(Y\) is an irreducible normal variety of dimension \(n\). Consider the composite morphism: \[ g := f \circ p: Y \longrightarrow \mathbb{P}^n, \quad \deg g = m \cdot d \ge 2d. \] Because \(d \le n\) and \(2d - 1 \ge d\), we have \(d \le \min\{\deg g - 1, \, n\}\). By Theorem 1 applied to \(g\), there exists a point \(y \in Y\) such that: \[ e_g(y) \ge d + 1. \] However, since \(p\) is étale, \(e_g(y) = e_p(y) \cdot e_f(p(y)) = 1 \cdot e_f(p(y)) \le d\), a contradiction. Thus \(X\) has no non-trivial connected finite étale covers, so \(\pi_1^{\text{ét}}(X) = \{e\}\).

Example 1 (Sharpness of the Degree Bound) The bound \(d \le n\) is sharp. Let \(E \subseteq \mathbb{P}^n\) be an elliptic curve of degree \(n + 1\) (for \(n \ge 2\), any degree \(n+1\) line bundle on \(E\) is very ample, and \(h^0(E, \mathcal{L}) = \deg \mathcal{L} + 1 - g = n + 1\)).

Define the incidence variety: \[ X := \big\{ (x, H) \in E \times \check{\mathbb{P}}^n \;\big|\; x \in H \big\} \subseteq E \times \check{\mathbb{P}}^n. \] The second projection \(p_2: X \to \check{\mathbb{P}}^n \cong \mathbb{P}^n\) is a finite covering of degree \(n + 1\). On the other hand, the first projection \(p_1: X \to E\) realizes \(X\) as a \(\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\)-bundle over \(E\). From the homotopy exact sequence: \[ \pi_1(\mathbb{P}^{n-1}) \longrightarrow \pi_1(X) \longrightarrow \pi_1(E) \longrightarrow \pi_0(\mathbb{P}^{n-1}) \implies \pi_1(X) \cong \pi_1(E) \cong \mathbb{Z}^2, \] so \(\pi_1^{\text{ét}}(X) \cong \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}^2 \neq \{e\}\).

Definition 3 (Relative Secant and Tangent Varieties) Let \(X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r\) be a smooth subvariety of dimension \(n\), and let \(V \subseteq X\) be a subvariety of dimension \(v\). 1. A linear subspace \(L \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r\) is tangent to \(X\) at \(x\) if \(x \in L \cap X\) and \(\mathbb{T}_x \subseteq L\). 2. Considering the incidence along \(V \times X \subseteq X \times X\) inside the blow-up \(\mathrm{Bl}_{\Delta}(X \times X) / S_2 \cong X^{[2]}\), the relative secant variety and relative tangent variety of \(X\) with respect to \(V\) are: \[ \operatorname{Sec}_V(X) := \bigcup_{x \in V, \, y \in X} \overline{xy}, \qquad \operatorname{Tan}_V(X) := \bigcup_{x \in V} \mathbb{T}_x. \] Clearly \(\operatorname{Tan}_V(X) \subseteq \operatorname{Sec}_V(X)\) and \(X \subseteq \operatorname{Sec}_V(X)\).

Proposition 2 (Dimension of Relative Secant and Tangent Varieties) Let \(X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r\) be a smooth irreducible subvariety of dimension \(n\), and let \(V \subseteq X\) be a subvariety of dimension \(v\). Then either: \[ \dim \operatorname{Sec}_V(X) = n + v + 1 \quad \text{and} \quad \dim \operatorname{Tan}_V(X) = n + v, \] or: \[ \operatorname{Tan}_V(X) = \operatorname{Sec}_V(X). \]

Proof. Suppose \(\operatorname{Tan}_V(X) \neq \operatorname{Sec}_V(X)\), and suppose for contradiction that \(\dim \operatorname{Tan}_V(X) = t < n + v\). A general linear space \(L \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r\) of codimension \(t + 1\) satisfies: \[ L \cap \operatorname{Sec}_V(X) \neq \varnothing \quad \text{and} \quad L \cap \operatorname{Tan}_V(X) = \varnothing. \] Projection away from \(L\) gives \(p: X \to \mathbb{P}^t\). Consider the product morphism: \[ F := p|_V \times p: V \times X \longrightarrow \mathbb{P}^t \times \mathbb{P}^t. \] Because \(\dim(V \times X) = n + v > t\), the Fulton–Hansen Connectedness Theorem implies that \(F^{-1}(\Delta_{\mathbb{P}^t})\) is connected.

Since \(L \cap \operatorname{Sec}_V(X) \neq \varnothing\), there exist \(v_0 \in V\) and \(x_0 \in X\) with \(x_0 \neq v_0\) such that the line \(\overline{v_0 x_0}\) intersects \(L\), meaning \(p(v_0) = p(x_0)\). Hence \((v_0, x_0) \in F^{-1}(\Delta_{\mathbb{P}^t}) \setminus \Delta_V\). By connectedness, there exists an irreducible component \(T \subseteq F^{-1}(\Delta_{\mathbb{P}^t})\) containing \((v_0, x_0)\) such that: \[ T \cap \Delta_V \neq \varnothing. \] Pick \((v^*, v^*) \in T \cap \Delta_V\), and choose a sequence \((v_k, x_k) \in T \setminus \Delta_V\) converging to \((v^*, v^*)\) with \(v_k \neq x_k\) and \(p(v_k) = p(x_k)\). Then the secant lines \(\overline{v_k x_k}\) meet \(L\). Since the incidence variety \(\mathcal{U}_L := \{ (x, y, l) \mid l \in \overline{xy} \cap L \}\) is proper over \(V \times X\), taking the limit as \(k \to \infty\) shows that the limiting line is tangent to \(X\) at \(v^*\) and intersects \(L\).

Thus \(\mathbb{T}_{v^*} \cap L \neq \varnothing\), which contradicts \(L \cap \operatorname{Tan}_V(X) = \varnothing\).

Theorem 2 (Zak’s Theorem on Tangencies) Let \(X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r\) be a smooth, non-degenerate irreducible subvariety of dimension \(n\). Let \(L \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r\) be a linear subspace of dimension \(k\) with \(n \le k \le r - 1\). Then the tangency locus: \[ \big\{ x \in X \;\big|\; \mathbb{T}_x \subseteq L \big\} \] has dimension: \[ \dim\big\{ x \in X \;\big|\; \mathbb{T}_x \subseteq L \big\} \le k - n. \] In particular, its dimension is at most \(\min\{k - n, \, n - 1\}\).

Proof. Suppose for contradiction that there exists an irreducible component \(V \subseteq \{ x \in X \mid \mathbb{T}_x \subseteq L \}\) of dimension: \[ v := \dim V \ge k + 1 - n. \] Then by definition, for every \(x \in V\), the embedded tangent space \(\mathbb{T}_x \subseteq L\), which implies: \[ \operatorname{Tan}_V(X) = \bigcup_{x \in V} \mathbb{T}_x \subseteq L. \] Thus \(\dim \operatorname{Tan}_V(X) \le \dim L = k \le v + n - 1\). By Proposition 2, we must have: \[ \operatorname{Tan}_V(X) = \operatorname{Sec}_V(X). \] However, \(X \subseteq \operatorname{Sec}_V(X) = \operatorname{Tan}_V(X) \subseteq L\), which forces \(X \subseteq L\). This contradicts the assumption that \(X\) is non-degenerate in \(\mathbb{P}^r\).

Corollary 2 (Consequences of Zak’s Theorem) Let \(X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r\) be a smooth, non-degenerate irreducible subvariety of dimension \(n\).

  1. Finiteness of the Gauss Map: The Gauss map \(\gamma: X \to \operatorname{Gr}(n, \mathbb{P}^r)\), \(x \mapsto \mathbb{T}_x\) is a finite morphism.
  2. Singularities of Hyperplane Sections: For any hyperplane \(H \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r\): \[ \dim \operatorname{Sing}(X \cap H) \le r - 1 - n. \] Consequently:
    • If \(2n \ge r + 1\), then \(X \cap H\) is reduced for general \(H\).
    • If \(2n \ge r + 2\), then \(X \cap H\) is normal for general \(H\).
  3. Dimension of the Dual Variety: Let \(\mathcal{P} := \{ (x, H) \in X \times \check{\mathbb{P}}^r \mid \mathbb{T}_x \subseteq H \}\). Then \(\mathcal{P}\) is a \(\mathbb{P}^{r - 1 - n}\)-bundle over \(X\) with \(\dim \mathcal{P} = r - 1\). The dual variety \(X^* := p_2(\mathcal{P}) \subseteq \check{\mathbb{P}}^r\) satisfies: \[ \dim X^* \ge n. \]

Proof.

  1. Setting \(k = n\) in Theorem 2, for any \(n\)-plane \(L\), \(\dim \{ x \in X \mid \mathbb{T}_x = L \} \le n - n = 0\). Hence every fiber of the Gauss map is zero-dimensional; being projective, \(\gamma\) is finite.

  2. A point \(x \in X \cap H\) is singular if and only if the differential \(d(s|_X)\) vanishes at \(x\), which is equivalent to \(\mathbb{T}_x \subseteq H\). Thus: \[ \operatorname{Sing}(X \cap H) = \big\{ x \in X \;\big|\; \mathbb{T}_x \subseteq H \big\}. \] Applying Zak’s Theorem with \(k = r - 1\), we obtain \(\dim \operatorname{Sing}(X \cap H) \le (r - 1) - n = r - 1 - n\).

    • If \(2n \ge r + 1\), then \(\dim \operatorname{Sing}(X \cap H) \le n - 2 = \dim(X \cap H) - 1\), so \(X \cap H\) is regular in codimension \(0\) (\(R_0\)). Since \(X\) is smooth (hence Cohen–Macaulay), the hypersurface section \(X \cap H\) is Cohen–Macaulay (\(S_1\)). By Serre’s criterion, \(R_0 + S_1\) implies that \(X \cap H\) is reduced.
    • If \(2n \ge r + 2\), then \(\dim \operatorname{Sing}(X \cap H) \le n - 3 = \dim(X \cap H) - 2\), so \(X \cap H\) satisfies \(R_1\). Together with \(S_2\), Serre’s criterion implies that \(X \cap H\) is normal.

  3. For any \(H \in X^*\), the fiber \(p_2^{-1}(H) \cong \{ x \in X \mid \mathbb{T}_x \subseteq H \}\) has dimension at most \(r - 1 - n\). By fiber dimension: \[ \dim X^* \ge \dim \mathcal{P} - (r - 1 - n) = (r - 1) - (r - 1 - n) = n. \]

Remark 1 (Join of Varieties). Replacing \(\operatorname{Hilb}_2(X)\) with the blow-up of \(X \times Y\) along the diagonal, one defines the join \(X * Y\) and the infinitesimal join \(\mathcal{L}\operatorname{Join}(X, Y)\). By identical arguments, either \(\dim(X * Y) = \dim X + \dim Y + 1\) and \(\dim \mathcal{L}\operatorname{Join}(X, Y) = \dim X + \dim Y\), or \(\mathcal{L}\operatorname{Join}(X, Y) = X * Y\).

Theorem 3 (Zak’s Theorem on Linear Normality (Form I)) Let \(X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r\) be a smooth projective variety of dimension \(n\). If: \[ 3n > 2(r - 1), \] then \(X\) is linearly normal, meaning the restriction map: \[ H^0(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^r}(1)) \longrightarrow H^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X(1)) \] is surjective.

Proposition 3 (Geometric Characterization of Linear Normality) For a smooth projective variety \(X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r\), the following are equivalent: 1. \(X\) is not linearly normal. 2. \(X\) is the projection of a non-degenerate embedding in \(\mathbb{P}^{r+1}\) from an external point \(P \notin X\). 3. There exists a non-degenerate embedding \(X \hookrightarrow \mathbb{P}^{r+1}\) such that \(\operatorname{Sec}(X) \subsetneq \mathbb{P}^{r+1}\).

Consequently, Theorem 3 is equivalent to:

Zak’s Theorem on Linear Normality (Form II): Let \(X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^m\) be a smooth, non-degenerate projective variety of dimension \(n\). If: \[ 3n > 2(m - 2), \] then: \[ \operatorname{Sec}(X) = \mathbb{P}^m. \]

Proof.

  • (1) \(\iff\) (2): The map \(H^0(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathcal{O}(1)) \to H^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X(1))\) fails to be surjective if and only if there exists a section \(f \in H^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X(1))\) not in the image. Choosing a basis \(s_0, \dots, s_r\) of \(H^0(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathcal{O}(1))\) and setting \(s_{r+1} := f\), the linear system \(\langle s_0, \dots, s_{r+1} \rangle\) defines an embedding \(X \hookrightarrow \mathbb{P}^{r+1}\) whose projection from \([0: \dots : 0: 1]\) recovers the original map to \(\mathbb{P}^r\).
  • (2) \(\iff\) (3): The projection from \(P \in \mathbb{P}^{r+1}\) restricts to an embedding on \(X\) if and only if \(P\) lies on no secant or tangent line of \(X\), which means \(P \notin \operatorname{Sec}(X)\). Such a point \(P\) exists if and only if \(\operatorname{Sec}(X) \subsetneq \mathbb{P}^{r+1}\).

Definition 4 (Trisecant and Higher Secant Varieties) Let \(X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^m\) be a smooth variety of dimension \(n\). A closed subscheme \(Z \subseteq X\) of dimension \(0\) and length \(3\) has local rings \(\mathcal{O}_Z = \prod A_i\) with \(\sum \operatorname{length}(A_i) = 3\): - Three distinct points: \(A_1 \times A_2 \times A_3\); - Two points (one tangent vector): \(A_1 \times A_2\) with \(\operatorname{length}(A_2) = 2\); - One point (length \(3\) local ring): \(\mathcal{O}_{X, x} / I\) where: - If \(\mathfrak{m} / \mathfrak{m}^2\) has image of dimension \(1\), \(I = (x_1^3, x_2, \dots, x_n)\), which defines an osculating plane \(\mathbb{P}^2 \subseteq T_x^{(2)} X\); - If \(\mathfrak{m} / \mathfrak{m}^2\) has image of dimension \(2\), \(I = (x_1^2, x_1 x_2, x_2^2, x_3, \dots, x_n)\), whose spanned plane is already contained in \(\mathbb{T}_x X \subseteq \operatorname{Sec}(X)\).

The trisecant variety is the variety: \[ \operatorname{Trisec}(X) := \operatorname{Sec}^3(X) = \bigcup_{Z \in \operatorname{Hilb}_3(X)} \operatorname{Span}(Z) = X * X * X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^m. \] More generally, the \(k\)-th secant variety is \(\operatorname{Sec}^k(X) := X * \dots * X\) (\(k\) times).

Remark 2 (Smoothness and Irreducibility of Hilbert Schemes of Points).

  • By Fogarty (1968), \(\operatorname{Hilb}_m(X)\) is smooth if and only if \(\dim X \le 2\) or \(m \le 3\).
  • \(\operatorname{Hilb}_m(X)\) is irreducible if \(\dim X \le 2\).
  • \(\operatorname{Hilb}_m(\mathbb{P}^3)\) is irreducible for \(m \le 7\) (Mozgovoy 2008), has two irreducible components for \(m = 8\) (Cartwright–Erman–Velasco–Viray 2008), while \(\operatorname{Hilb}_{11}(\mathbb{P}^3)\) is irreducible (DJUNT 2017).

Lemma 2 (Terracini’s Lemma) Let \(X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^m\) be a smooth, irreducible subvariety. 1. For distinct points \(x, y \in X\) and \(p \in \overline{xy}\): \[ \operatorname{Span}(\mathbb{T}_x, \, \mathbb{T}_y) \subseteq T_p \operatorname{Sec}(X), \] with equality holding for general \(x, y \in X\) and general \(p \in \overline{xy}\). 2. For non-collinear points \(x, y, z \in X\) and \(p \in \overline{xyz}\): \[ \operatorname{Span}(\mathbb{T}_x, \, \mathbb{T}_y, \, \mathbb{T}_z) \subseteq T_p \operatorname{Trisec}(X), \] with equality holding for general points.

Corollary 3 (Secant Equals Trisecant) Let \(X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^m\) be a smooth, non-degenerate projective variety. Then: \[ \operatorname{Sec}(X) = \mathbb{P}^m \iff \operatorname{Sec}(X) = \operatorname{Trisec}(X). \]

Proof. The implication \((\implies)\) is immediate since \(\operatorname{Sec}(X) \subseteq \operatorname{Trisec}(X) \subseteq \mathbb{P}^m\).

Conversely, suppose \(\operatorname{Sec}(X) \subsetneq \mathbb{P}^m\). For general \(x, y \in X\) and general \(p \in \overline{xy}\), Terracini’s Lemma gives \(T_p \operatorname{Sec}(X) = \operatorname{Span}(\mathbb{T}_x, \mathbb{T}_y) =: \Lambda \subsetneq \mathbb{P}^m\). Because \(X\) is non-degenerate, \(X \nsubseteq \Lambda\). Choosing \(z \in X \setminus \Lambda\), we have: \[ \operatorname{Span}(\mathbb{T}_x, \mathbb{T}_y, \mathbb{T}_z) \supsetneq \operatorname{Span}(\mathbb{T}_x, \mathbb{T}_y), \] which implies \(\dim \operatorname{Trisec}(X) > \dim \operatorname{Sec}(X)\), so \(\operatorname{Sec}(X) \neq \operatorname{Trisec}(X)\).

Proposition 4 (Proof of Linear Normality (Form II)) Let \(X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^m\) be a smooth projective variety of dimension \(n\). If: \[ 3n > 2(m - 2), \] then \(\operatorname{Sec}(X) = \operatorname{Trisec}(X)\), and consequently \(\operatorname{Sec}(X) = \mathbb{P}^m\).

Proof. Suppose for contradiction that \(\operatorname{Sec}(X) \subsetneq \operatorname{Trisec}(X)\). Then there exist points \(x_0, y_0, z_0 \in X\) spanning a plane \(\overline{x_0 y_0 z_0} \cong \mathbb{P}^2\) not contained in \(\operatorname{Sec}(X)\). Thus: \[ \operatorname{Sec}(X) \cap \overline{x_0 y_0 z_0} \subsetneq \mathbb{P}^2 \] is a proper closed algebraic subset, hence has dimension \(\le 1\). In particular, \(X \cap \overline{x_0 y_0 z_0}\) consists of only finitely many points (otherwise, any curve in the plane would force its secants to fill the entire \(\mathbb{P}^2\)).

Choose a general line \(L \subseteq \overline{x_0 y_0 z_0}\). Then \(L\) meets \(\operatorname{Sec}(X)\) transversely in finitely many points, and \(L \cap X = \varnothing\). Projection away from \(L\) defines a finite morphism: \[ \pi: X \longrightarrow \mathbb{P}^{m-2}. \] Consider the triple product map: \[ F := \pi \times \pi \times \pi: X \times X \times X \longrightarrow \mathbb{P}^{m-2} \times \mathbb{P}^{m-2} \times \mathbb{P}^{m-2}. \] Because \(\dim(X \times X \times X) = 3n > 2(m - 2)\), Fulton–Hansen Connectedness implies that: \[ F^{-1}(\Delta_{\mathbb{P}^{m-2}}) = \big\{ (x, y, z) \in X^3 \;\big|\; \pi(x) = \pi(y) = \pi(z) \big\} \] is connected. Geometrically, \(\pi(x) = \pi(y) = \pi(z)\) means that the secant lines \(\overline{xy}\), \(\overline{xz}\), and \(\overline{yz}\) all meet the line \(L\).

Define the continuous map: \[ \Phi: F^{-1}(\Delta_{\mathbb{P}^{m-2}}) \setminus D_X \longrightarrow L \times L \times L, \quad (x, y, z) \longmapsto (\overline{yz} \cap L, \; \overline{xz} \cap L, \; \overline{xy} \cap L), \] where \(D_X := (\Delta_X \times X) \cup (X \times \Delta_X) \cup (\Delta_{1,3})\). Because \(L \cap \operatorname{Sec}(X)\) is a finite set of points, the image of \(\Phi\) is finite, so \(\Phi\) is locally constant.

By the connectedness of \(F^{-1}(\Delta_{\mathbb{P}^{m-2}})\), \(\Phi\) must be constant on every component: \[ \Phi(x, y, z) = (a, b, c) \in L^3, \] where \(a, b, c\) are distinct points on \(L\) (if, say, \(a = b\), then \(\overline{xy}\) and \(\overline{xz}\) meet \(L\) at the same point, which forces \(x, y, z\) to be collinear, contradicting \(L \cap X = \varnothing\)).

Now take a smooth curve \(T\) in an irreducible component of \(F^{-1}(\Delta_{\mathbb{P}^{m-2}})\) specializing a general point \((x_t, y_t, z_t)\) to a point \((x^*, y^*, z^*) \in D_X\) at \(t = t^*\): - If \(y^* = z^* \neq x^*\), then the two secant lines \(\overline{x_t y_t}\) and \(\overline{x_t z_t}\) both degenerate to the single line \(\overline{x^* y^*}\), forcing their intersection points \(a\) and \(b\) on \(L\) to coincide, a contradiction. - If \(x^* = y^* = z^*\), then by the valuative criterion, the limits of \(\overline{x_t y_t}\), \(\overline{x_t z_t}\), and \(\overline{y_t z_t}\) are three distinct tangent lines of \(X\) at \(x^*\) meeting \(L\) at \(a, b, c\). This implies that \(L \subseteq \mathbb{T}_{x^*} X \subseteq \operatorname{Sec}(X)\), which contradicts the fact that \(L \cap \operatorname{Sec}(X)\) is finite.

This contradiction establishes that \(\operatorname{Sec}(X) = \operatorname{Trisec}(X)\), and thus \(\operatorname{Sec}(X) = \mathbb{P}^m\).

Theorem 4 (Branching over Nodal Curves (Zariski’s Problem)) Let \(C \subseteq \mathbb{P}^2\) be a (possibly reducible) plane curve having only nodal singularities (i.e., at every singular point \(p \in C\), the formal completion satisfies \(\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{C, p} \cong k[[x, y]] / (xy)\)).

If \(f: X \to \mathbb{P}^2\) is a finite covering branched only along \(C\) (i.e., \(B(f) \subseteq C\)), then: 1. \(f\) is a Galois covering; 2. The Galois group \(\operatorname{Gal}(X / \mathbb{P}^2)\) is abelian.
