22.8 Analytic Topology

Morse Theory, Andreotti-Frankel Theorem, Lefschetz Hyperplane Theorem, and Barth’s Theorem

Published

October 8, 2026

Example 1 (Homotopy Type of Stein Manifolds) Consider the closed complex submanifold of \(\mathbb{C}^2\) defined by: \[ V = V\big(w \sin(\pi z) - 1\big) = \left\{ (z, w) \in \mathbb{C}^2 \;\middle|\; w \sin(\pi z) = 1 \right\} \subseteq \mathbb{C}^2. \] Then \(V\) is biholomorphic to the punctured complex plane \(\mathbb{C} \setminus \mathbb{Z}\) via the biholomorphism: \[ V \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;} \mathbb{C} \setminus \mathbb{Z}, \quad (z, w) \longmapsto z, \qquad z \longmapsto \left(z, \frac{1}{\sin(\pi z)}\right). \] Topologically, \(\mathbb{C} \setminus \mathbb{Z}\) deformation retracts onto a countable wedge of circles: \[ V \simeq \mathbb{C} \setminus \mathbb{Z} \simeq \bigvee_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} S^1. \] In particular, \(V\) has the homotopy type of a \(1\)-dimensional CW complex (real dimension \(1\), while \(\dim_{\mathbb{C}} V = 1\)). Its singular homology and cohomology satisfy: \[ H^1_{\mathrm{sing}}(V, \mathbb{Z}) \cong \bigoplus_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}, \qquad H^i_{\mathrm{sing}}(V, \mathbb{Z}) = 0 \quad \text{for all } i \ge 2. \]

Question: Is every Stein manifold (in particular, any closed complex submanifold of \(\mathbb{C}^N\)) of complex dimension \(n\) homotopy equivalent to a CW complex of real dimension at most \(n\)?

Answer: Yes! This is the celebrated Andreotti-Frankel Theorem (Theorem 3).

Definition 1 (Morse Functions and Exhaustion Functions) Let \(M \subseteq \mathbb{R}^N\) be a smooth real manifold of dimension \(m \ge 2\), and let \(f \in C^\infty(M, \mathbb{R})\).

  1. \(f\) is called an exhaustion function if for every \(a \in \mathbb{R}\), the sublevel set \(f^{-1}((-\infty, a])\) is compact in \(M\).
  2. A point \(p \in M\) is a critical point of \(f\) if \(df_p = 0\). The set of critical points is denoted by \(\operatorname{Crit}(f)\).
  3. A critical point \(p \in \operatorname{Crit}(f)\) is called nondegenerate if the Hessian matrix: \[ \operatorname{Hess}_p(f) = \left( \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}(p) \right) \] is nonsingular (nondegenerate) in local coordinates.
  4. \(f\) is called a Morse function if every critical point \(p \in \operatorname{Crit}(f)\) is nondegenerate.
  5. For a nondegenerate critical point \(p \in \operatorname{Crit}(f)\), the index of \(f\) at \(p\) is defined as: \[ \operatorname{Index}_p(f) := \# \big\{ \lambda < 0 \;\big|\; \lambda \text{ is an eigenvalue of } \operatorname{Hess}_p(f) \big\}, \] counted with multiplicity.

Theorem 1 (Existence of Distance Morse Functions) Let \(M \subseteq \mathbb{R}^N\) be a smooth closed submanifold of dimension \(m \ge 2\). For any \(c \in \mathbb{R}^N\), consider the squared Euclidean distance function: \[ \phi_c: M \longrightarrow \mathbb{R}, \quad x \longmapsto \|x - c\|^2. \] Then \(\phi_c\) is an exhaustion function, and for almost all \(c \in \mathbb{R}^N\) (outside a subset of Lebesgue measure zero), \(\phi_c\) is a Morse function.

Theorem 2 (CW Complex from an Exhaustion Morse Function) Let \(M\) be a smooth manifold and let \(f: M \to \mathbb{R}\) be an exhaustion Morse function. Then \(M\) has the homotopy type of a CW complex \(K\): \[ M \simeq K, \] where the cells of \(K\) are in one-to-one correspondence with the critical points of \(f\): \[ \{\text{cells of } K\} \stackrel{1:1}{\longleftrightarrow} \operatorname{Crit}(f), \quad e_p^m \longleftrightarrow p \in \operatorname{Crit}(f) \text{ with } m = \operatorname{Index}_p(f). \]

Theorem 3 (Andreotti-Frankel Theorem) Let \(V \subseteq \mathbb{C}^r\) be a connected complex submanifold of complex dimension \(n\). Then \(V\) has the homotopy type of a CW complex \(K\) of real dimension at most \(n\): \[ V \simeq K \quad \text{with} \quad \dim_{\mathbb{R}} K \le n. \] In particular, the singular cohomology of \(V\) vanishes above degree \(n\): \[ H^i_{\mathrm{sing}}(V, \mathbb{Z}) = 0 \quad \text{for all } i > n. \]

Proof. By Theorem 1, we can choose a point \(c \in \mathbb{R}^{2r} \cong \mathbb{C}^r\) such that \(\phi_c: V \to \mathbb{R}\), \(x \mapsto \|x - c\|^2\), is an exhaustion Morse function on \(V\).

By Theorem 2, to show that \(\dim_{\mathbb{R}} K \le n\), it suffices to show that: \[ \operatorname{Index}_p(\phi_c) \le n \quad \text{for every critical point } p \in \operatorname{Crit}(\phi_c). \]

Fix \(p \in \operatorname{Crit}(\phi_c)\). Because the question is purely local around \(p\), after a unitary transformation and translation on \(\mathbb{C}^r\), we may assume without loss of generality that: \[ p = 0 \in \mathbb{C}^r \quad \text{and} \quad T_0 V = \operatorname{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}\left( \frac{\partial}{\partial z_1}, \dots, \frac{\partial}{\partial z_n} \right) \subseteq \mathbb{C}^r. \]

Step 1: Local chart parametrization. Near \(0\), \(V\) is represented as the graph of a holomorphic mapping over an open neighborhood \(U_1 \subseteq \mathbb{C}^n\) of the origin: \[ g: U_1 \longrightarrow V \subseteq \mathbb{C}^r, \quad z = (z_1, \dots, z_n) \longmapsto \big(z_1, \dots, z_n, f_1(z), \dots, f_k(z)\big), \] where \(k = r - n\), \(f_l(0) = 0\), and \(df_l(0) = 0\) for each \(1 \le l \le k\). Thus: \[ dg_0 = \begin{pmatrix} I_n \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \operatorname{Im}(dg_0) = T_0 V. \]

Step 2: Characterization of the critical point \(c\). The differential \((d\phi_c)_0: T_0 V \to \mathbb{R}\) vanishes on any \(v \in T_0 V\). Let \(w = dg_0^{-1}(v) \in \mathbb{C}^n\). Differentiating along the real line \(t \mapsto t w\) gives: \[ \begin{aligned} 0 = \left. \frac{d}{dt} \right|_{t=0} \|g(tw) - c\|^2 &= \left. \frac{d}{dt} \right|_{t=0} (g(tw) - c)^T \overline{(g(tw) - c)} \\ &= (dg_0(w))^T \overline{(-c)} + (-c)^T \overline{dg_0(w)} \\ &= (w_1, \dots, w_n, 0, \dots, 0)^T (-\bar{c}) + (-c)^T \overline{(w_1, \dots, w_n, 0, \dots, 0)} \\ &= -2 \operatorname{Re} \langle v, c \rangle. \end{aligned} \] Replacing \(v\) with \(i v \in T_0 V\) yields \(\operatorname{Im} \langle v, c \rangle = 0\), hence \(\langle v, c \rangle = 0\) for all \(v \in T_0 V\). Thus \(c \in (T_0 V)^\perp\).

Applying an invertible unitary change of coordinates on the normal space \(\mathbb{C}^k\) (leaving \(T_0 V\) invariant), we may arrange that: \[ c = (0, \dots, 0, 1, 0, \dots, 0) \in \mathbb{C}^n \times \mathbb{C}^k, \] where \(1\) is placed in the \((n+1)\)-th coordinate (the first coordinate of \(\mathbb{C}^k\)).

Step 3: Pullback and Hessian calculation. Pulling back \(\phi_c\) via the chart \(g\) yields: \[ \begin{aligned} \phi_c(z) &= |z_1|^2 + \dots + |z_n|^2 + |f_1(z) - 1|^2 + |f_2(z)|^2 + \dots + |f_k(z)|^2 \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^n |z_j|^2 + \sum_{l=1}^k |f_l(z)|^2 - 2 \operatorname{Re}\big(f_1(z)\big) + 1. \end{aligned} \] Since \(f_l(0) = 0\) and \(df_l(0) = 0\), the Hessian of each \(|f_l|^2\) at \(0\) vanishes: \[ \operatorname{Hess}_0\big(|f_l|^2\big) = 2 \operatorname{Re}(f_l) \operatorname{Hess}_0\big(\operatorname{Re} f_l\big) + 2 \operatorname{Im}(f_l) \operatorname{Hess}_0\big(\operatorname{Im} f_l\big) = 0. \] Therefore, the Hessian of \(\phi_c\) at the origin simplifies to: \[ \operatorname{Hess}_0(\phi_c) = I_{2n} - 2 \operatorname{Hess}_0\big(\operatorname{Re} f_1\big). \]

Step 4: Eigenvalue pairing of the holomorphic Hessian. Since \(f_1\) is holomorphic with \(f_1(0) = 0\) and \(df_1(0) = 0\), its Taylor expansion at \(0\) is: \[ f_1(z) = Q(z) + O(|z|^3), \] where \(Q(z) = z^T C z\) is a complex symmetric quadratic form with \(C = A + iB\), where \(A, B \in \operatorname{Sym}_n(\mathbb{R})\). Writing \(z = x + iy\) with \(x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n\): \[ \operatorname{Re} Q(x + iy) = x^T A x - y^T A y - 2 x^T B y = \begin{pmatrix} x^T & y^T \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} A & -B \\ -B & -A \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}. \] Let \(H = \begin{pmatrix} A & -B \\ -B & -A \end{pmatrix}\) and let \(J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -I_n \\ I_n & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) be the standard complex structure. A direct matrix computation shows: \[ J H = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -I_n \\ I_n & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} A & -B \\ -B & -A \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} B & A \\ A & -B \end{pmatrix} = - H J. \] Therefore, if \(\begin{pmatrix} u \\ v \end{pmatrix}\) is an eigenvector of \(H\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\), then: \[ H \left( \begin{pmatrix} -v \\ u \end{pmatrix} \right) = H J \begin{pmatrix} u \\ v \end{pmatrix} = - J H \begin{pmatrix} u \\ v \end{pmatrix} = -\lambda J \begin{pmatrix} u \\ v \end{pmatrix} = -\lambda \begin{pmatrix} -v \\ u \end{pmatrix}. \] Hence the eigenvalues of \(H = \operatorname{Hess}_0(\operatorname{Re} Q)\) occur in opposite pairs \(\{\lambda, -\lambda\}\). In particular, \(H\) can have at most \(n\) strictly positive eigenvalues: \[ \# \big\{ \text{positive eigenvalues of } \operatorname{Hess}_0(\operatorname{Re} Q) \big\} \le n. \]

Step 5: Bounding the Morse index. The eigenvalues of \(\operatorname{Hess}_0(\phi_c) = I_{2n} - 2 H\) are of the form \(1 - 2\lambda\), where \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(H\). An eigenvalue \(1 - 2\lambda\) is strictly negative if and only if: \[ 1 - 2\lambda < 0 \iff \lambda > \frac{1}{2} > 0. \] Because at most \(n\) eigenvalues of \(H\) are positive, at most \(n\) eigenvalues satisfy \(\lambda > 1/2\). Consequently: \[ \operatorname{Index}_0(\phi_c) = \#\{ 1 - 2\lambda < 0 \} \le n. \] This completes the proof.

Corollary 1 (Smooth Affine Varieties Have Finite Homotopy Type) Let \(V \subseteq \mathbb{C}^r\) be a smooth affine algebraic variety of dimension \(n\). Then in the analytic topology, \(V^{\mathrm{an}}\) has the homotopy type of a finite CW complex of dimension at most \(n\).

Proof. Let \(V = V(f_1, \dots, f_k) \subseteq \mathbb{C}^r\). By separating each polynomial and coordinate into real and imaginary parts, \(V\) can be viewed as a real algebraic subvariety: \[ V = V(g_1, \dots, g_p) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{2r}. \] For any \(x \in V\), the normal space is \(N_x V = \operatorname{Span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{\nabla g_1(x), \dots, \nabla g_p(x)\}\).

Let \(\phi_c(x) = \|x - c\|^2\) be a Morse exhaustion function on \(V\). A point \(q \in V\) is a critical point of \(\phi_c|_V\) if and only if \(c - q \in N_q V\), which is equivalent to: \[ \operatorname{rank}\big(\nabla g_1(q), \dots, \nabla g_p(q) \;\big|\; q - c\big) \le 2r - 2n. \] This condition is algebraic: it is cut out by the vanishing of all \((2r - 2n + 1) \times (2r - 2n + 1)\) minors of the augmented matrix. Hence \(\operatorname{Crit}(\phi_c)\) is a real algebraic subvariety of \(V\).

On the other hand, since \(\phi_c\) is a Morse function, all its critical points are nondegenerate, meaning the differential \(D(\nabla \phi_c|_V) = \operatorname{Hess}(\phi_c)\) is invertible at each critical point. By the inverse function theorem, \(\operatorname{Crit}(\phi_c)\) consists of isolated points, so \(\dim_{\mathbb{R}} \operatorname{Crit}(\phi_c) = 0\).

A \(0\)-dimensional real algebraic variety in \(\mathbb{R}^{2r}\) must be a finite set. Therefore, \(\operatorname{Crit}(\phi_c)\) is finite, and by Theorem 2, \(V^{\mathrm{an}}\) is homotopy equivalent to a finite CW complex of dimension \(\le n\).

Theorem 4 (Artin-Grothendieck Vanishing Theorem) Let \(V\) be an affine variety over \(\mathbb{C}\) of dimension \(n\), and let \(\mathcal{F}\) be an algebraically constructible sheaf on \(V\) (that is, \(\mathcal{F}\) is analytically locally constant on each stratum of some Zariski stratification \(V = \bigsqcup X_i\)). Then: \[ H^i(V, \mathcal{F}) = 0 \quad \text{for all } i > n. \]

Theorem 5 (Lefschetz Hyperplane Theorem) Let \(X\) be an \(n\)-dimensional nonsingular irreducible complex projective variety, and let \(D \subseteq X\) be an effective ample divisor. Then: \[ H^i(X, D; \mathbb{Z}) = 0 \quad \text{for all } i \le n - 1. \] Consequently, the restriction homomorphism \(H^i(X, \mathbb{Z}) \to H^i(D, \mathbb{Z})\) is:

  1. an isomorphism for all \(i \le n - 2\);
  2. injective for \(i = n - 1\).

Proof. Because \(\operatorname{Supp}(D) = \operatorname{Supp}(mD)\), we may replace \(D\) by a large multiple \(m D\) and assume without loss of generality that \(D\) is very ample, cut out by a hyperplane section \(H\). The complement: \[ X \setminus D = X \setminus (X \cap H) \] is an affine variety of complex dimension \(n\). By the Andreotti-Frankel Theorem (Theorem 3) or Artin-Grothendieck vanishing (Theorem 4): \[ H_i(X \setminus D, \mathbb{Z}) = 0 \quad \text{for all } i > n. \]

Lemma 1 (Lefschetz Duality) Consider the long exact sequence of compactly supported cohomology and relative cohomology: \[ \begin{CD} H_c^k(X \setminus D) @>>> H^k(X) @>>> H^k(D) @>>> H_c^{k+1}(X \setminus D) \\ @V{\cong}VV @| @| \\ H^k(X, D) @>>> H^k(X) @>>> H^k(D) \end{CD} \] By Poincaré-Lefschetz duality on the \(2n\)-dimensional real manifold \(X\): \[ H_i(X \setminus D, \mathbb{Z}) = 0 \iff H_c^{2n - i}(X \setminus D, \mathbb{Z}) = 0 \iff H^{2n - i}(X, D; \mathbb{Z}) = 0. \] Setting \(k = 2n - i\), the condition \(i > n\) is equivalent to \(0 \le k \le n - 1\). Hence: \[ H^k(X, D; \mathbb{Z}) = 0 \quad \text{for all } 0 \le k \le n - 1. \]

Now examine the long exact sequence of the pair \((X, D)\): \[ H^{i-1}(D, \mathbb{Z}) \longrightarrow H^i(X, D; \mathbb{Z}) \longrightarrow H^i(X, \mathbb{Z}) \longrightarrow H^i(D, \mathbb{Z}) \longrightarrow H^{i+1}(X, D; \mathbb{Z}). \] - For \(i \le n - 2\), both \(H^i(X, D; \mathbb{Z}) = 0\) and \(H^{i+1}(X, D; \mathbb{Z}) = 0\), so \(H^i(X, \mathbb{Z}) \xrightarrow{\sim} H^i(D, \mathbb{Z})\) is an isomorphism. - For \(i = n - 1\), \(H^{n-1}(X, D; \mathbb{Z}) = 0\), so \(H^{n-1}(X, \mathbb{Z}) \hookrightarrow H^{n-1}(D, \mathbb{Z})\) is injective.

Corollary 2 (Connectedness and Picard Group of Ample Divisors) Let \(X\) be a smooth projective variety over \(\mathbb{C}\).

  1. If \(\dim X \ge 2\) and \(D \subseteq X\) is an effective ample divisor, then \(D\) is connected in both the classical (analytic) and Zariski topologies.
  2. If \(\dim X \ge 3\) and \(D \subseteq X\) is a reduced effective ample divisor, then the restriction map induces an isomorphism on Picard groups: \[ \operatorname{Pic}(X) \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;} \operatorname{Pic}(D). \]

Proof. Proof of (1): Since \(n = \dim X \ge 2\), we have \(n - 2 \ge 0\). By Theorem 5: \[ H^0(X, \mathbb{Z}) \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;} H^0(D, \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}. \] Therefore, \(D\) is connected in the classical analytic topology. By Serre’s GAGA, \(H^0(D, \mathcal{O}_D) \cong H^0(D^{\mathrm{an}}, \mathcal{O}_{D^{\mathrm{an}}})\) has no nontrivial idempotents, which proves \(D\) is also connected in the Zariski topology.

Proof of (2): Since \(D\) is reduced, we have the exponential sheaf sequence \(0 \to \mathbb{Z} \to \mathcal{O}_D \to \mathcal{O}_D^* \to 0\) on \(D\), and similarly on \(X\). Consider the commutative diagram of long exact sequences: \[ \begin{CD} H^1(X, \mathbb{Z}) @>>> H^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X) @>>> H^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X^*) @>>> H^2(X, \mathbb{Z}) @>>> H^2(X, \mathcal{O}_X) \\ @VV{\sim}V @VV{\operatorname{res}}V @VV{\operatorname{res}}V @VV{\sim}V @VV{\operatorname{res}}V \\ H^1(D, \mathbb{Z}) @>>> H^1(D, \mathcal{O}_D) @>>> H^1(D, \mathcal{O}_D^*) @>>> H^2(D, \mathbb{Z}) @>>> H^2(D, \mathcal{O}_D) \end{CD} \] From the short exact sequence of ideal sheaves \(0 \to \mathcal{O}_X(-D) \to \mathcal{O}_X \to \mathcal{O}_D \to 0\), we have: \[ H^i(X, \mathcal{O}_X(-D)) \longrightarrow H^i(X, \mathcal{O}_X) \longrightarrow H^i(D, \mathcal{O}_D) \longrightarrow H^{i+1}(X, \mathcal{O}_X(-D)). \] By Kodaira vanishing and Serre duality, \(H^j(X, \mathcal{O}_X(-D)) \cong H^{n-j}(X, K_X + D)^\vee = 0\) for all \(j < n\). Since \(n \ge 3\), this vanishing holds for \(j = 1, 2\), which implies: \[ H^i(X, \mathcal{O}_X) \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;} H^i(D, \mathcal{O}_D) \quad \text{for } i \le n - 2. \] Since \(n \ge 3\), this is an isomorphism for \(i = 1\) (and for \(i = 2\) when \(n \ge 4\); when \(n = 3\), it is injective). Furthermore, by Theorem 5: - \(H^1(X, \mathbb{Z}) \xrightarrow{\sim} H^1(D, \mathbb{Z})\) is an isomorphism (as \(1 \le n - 2\)); - \(H^2(X, \mathbb{Z}) \to H^2(D, \mathbb{Z})\) is an isomorphism for \(n \ge 4\), and injective for \(n = 3\).

By the Five Lemma, the restriction map on Picard groups: \[ \operatorname{Pic}(X) \cong H^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X^*) \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;} H^1(D, \mathcal{O}_D^*) \cong \operatorname{Pic}(D) \] is an isomorphism.

Corollary 3  

  1. Let \(X\) be a smooth projective variety of dimension \(n\), and let \(D \subseteq X\) be a nonsingular ample effective divisor. The restriction map on Hodge cohomology: \[ \operatorname{res}: H^p(X, \Omega_X^q) \longrightarrow H^p(D, \Omega_D^q) \] is an isomorphism for \(p + q \le n - 2\), and injective for \(p + q = n - 1\).
  2. Let \(F \in \mathbb{C}[x_0, \dots, x_n]\) be an irreducible homogeneous polynomial with \(n \ge 4\). If the projective hypersurface \(D = V(F) \subseteq \mathbb{P}^n\) is nonsingular, then the coordinate ring: \[ R = \mathbb{C}[x_0, \dots, x_n] / (F) \] is a Unique Factorization Domain (UFD).

Proof. Proof of (2): An integral domain \(R\) is a UFD if and only if \(R\) is normal and its divisor class group vanishes: \(\operatorname{Cl}(R) = 0\).

  • Normality: Because \(D = V(F)\) is smooth in \(\mathbb{P}^n\), normal ci is projectively normal. Then the affine cone i.e. \(R\) is normal.
  • Divisor Class Group: For the punctured affine cone \(\operatorname{Spec} R \setminus \{0\}\), the divisor class group satisfies: \[ \operatorname{Cl}(R) \cong \operatorname{Pic}(\operatorname{Spec} R \setminus \{0\}). \] The punctured cone \(\operatorname{Spec} R \setminus \{0\}\) is the total space of the \(\mathbb{C}^*\)-bundle associated with \(\mathcal{O}_D(-1)\) without its zero section, fitting into the fibration: \[ \begin{CD} \operatorname{Spec} R \setminus \{0\} = \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^n}(-1)|_D \setminus \{0\} @>>> \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^n}(-1)|_D @>>> D \\ @VVV @VVV @VVV \\ \mathbb{A}^{n+1} \setminus \{0\} = \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^n}(-1) \setminus \{0\} @>>> \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^n}(-1) @>>> \mathbb{P}^n \end{CD} \] From the vector bundle homotopy invariance, \(\operatorname{Pic}(\mathcal{O}_D(-1)) \cong \operatorname{Pic}(D)\), and removing the zero section yields the exact sequence: \[ 0 \longrightarrow \mathbb{Z} \cdot [\mathcal{O}_D(-1)] \longrightarrow \operatorname{Pic}(D) \longrightarrow \operatorname{Pic}(\operatorname{Spec} R \setminus \{0\}) \longrightarrow 0. \] Since \(n \ge 4 \ge 3\), by Corollary 2: \[ \operatorname{Pic}(D) \cong \operatorname{Pic}(\mathbb{P}^n) \cong \mathbb{Z} \cdot [\mathcal{O}_D(1)]. \] Because \([\mathcal{O}_D(-1)]\) generates \(\operatorname{Pic}(D)\), the quotient is trivial: \[ \operatorname{Cl}(R) \cong \operatorname{Pic}(\operatorname{Spec} R \setminus \{0\}) = 0. \] Hence \(R\) is a UFD.

Theorem 6 (Barth’s Theorem) Let \(X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r\) be a nonsingular complex subvariety of dimension \(n\) and codimension \(e = r - n\). Then the restriction homomorphism: \[ j^*: H^i(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathbb{C}) \longrightarrow H^i(X, \mathbb{C}) \] is an isomorphism for all \(i \le n - e = 2n - r\).

Proof. Let \(j: X \hookrightarrow \mathbb{P}^r\) denote the inclusion. Let \(\zeta = c_1(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^r}(1)) \in H^2(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathbb{C})\) be the hyperplane class, so that: \[ H^*(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathbb{C}) \cong \mathbb{C}[\zeta] / (\zeta^{r+1}), \quad \deg \zeta = 2. \] Let \(d = (X \cdot H^e)\) be the degree of \(X\).

Step 1: Fundamental class and degree. The fundamental class \(\eta_X \in H^{2e}(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathbb{C})\) is Poincaré dual to the cycle \([X] \in H_{2n}(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathbb{C})\). Since \(H^{2e}(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{C} \cdot \zeta^e\), we can write \(\eta_X = a \zeta^e\). Evaluating on \(\zeta^n\) gives: \[ a = \int_{\mathbb{P}^r} \eta_X \cup \zeta^n = \eta_X(\zeta^n) = \int_X j^* \zeta^n = (X \cdot H^n) = d. \] Thus \(\eta_X = d \cdot \zeta^e\).

Step 2: The Gysin homomorphism. The Gysin pushforward map \(j_*: H^i(X, \mathbb{C}) \to H^{i+2e}(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathbb{C})\) is defined via Poincaré duality: \[ j_*: H^i(X) \xrightarrow{\;\mathrm{PD}\;} H_{2n-i}(X) \xrightarrow{\;j_*\;} H_{2n-i}(\mathbb{P}^r) \xrightarrow{\;\mathrm{PD}\;} H^{i+2e}(\mathbb{P}^r). \] The pushforward and pullback satisfy the following two relations: 1. For any \(x \in H^i(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathbb{C})\), the projection formula gives: \[ j_*(j^*(x)) = x \cup \eta_X = d \cdot (x \cup \zeta^e). \] Hence the composition: \[ H^i(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathbb{C}) \xrightarrow{\;j^* \;} H^i(X, \mathbb{C}) \xrightarrow{\;j_*\;} H^{i+2e}(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathbb{C}) \] is multiplication by \(d \zeta^e\). Because \(H^*(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathbb{C})\) is generated by \(\zeta\), multiplication by \(\zeta^e\) is an isomorphism \(H^i(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathbb{C}) \xrightarrow{\sim} H^{i+2e}(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathbb{C})\) for all \(i \le 2(r - e) = 2n\). In particular, for \(i \le n - e\), the composition \(j_* \circ j^*\) is an isomorphism. This implies: - \(j_*\) is surjective; - \(j^*\) is injective.

  1. For any \(y \in H^i(X, \mathbb{C})\), the pullback of the pushforward satisfies: \[ j^*(j_*(y)) = y \cup j^*(\eta_X) = d \cdot \big(y \cup (j^* \zeta)^e\big). \] Consider the commutative diagram: \[ \begin{CD} H^i(\mathbb{P}^r) @>{j^*}>> H^i(X) \\ @V{\cdot d \zeta^e}VV @VV{\cdot d (j^*\zeta)^e}V \\ H^{i+2e}(\mathbb{P}^r) @>>{j^*}> H^{i+2e}(X) \end{CD} \] By the Hard Lefschetz Theorem on the smooth projective variety \(X\) of dimension \(n\), the Lefschetz operator \(L = \cup (j^* \zeta)\) induces an isomorphism: \[ L^k: H^{n-k}(X, \mathbb{C}) \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;} H^{n+k}(X, \mathbb{C}). \] For \(i \le n - e\), writing \(k = n - i \ge e\), the map \(L^k = L^{k-e} \circ L^e\) is an isomorphism, which forces the factor: \[ L^e: H^i(X, \mathbb{C}) \longrightarrow H^{i+2e}(X, \mathbb{C}) \] to be injective. Therefore, \(j^* \circ j_* = d \cdot L^e\) is injective, which implies that \(j_*\) is injective.

Step 3: Conclusion. From (1), \(j_*\) is surjective; from (2), \(j_*\) is injective. Hence \(j_*\) is an isomorphism. Because \(j_* \circ j^*\) is an isomorphism, the restriction map: \[ j^*: H^i(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathbb{C}) \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;} H^i(X, \mathbb{C}) \] is an isomorphism for all \(i \le n - e\).

Corollary 4 (Picard Group of Subvarieties of Small Codimension) Let \(X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^r\) be a smooth subvariety of dimension \(n\). If \(2n - r \ge 2\), then the restriction map induces an isomorphism: \[ \operatorname{Pic}(\mathbb{P}^r) \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;} \operatorname{Pic}(X) \cong \mathbb{Z}. \]

Proof. By Barth’s Theorem on \(\mathbb{Z}\) (Deep Fact), \(2n - r = n - e \ge 2\) implies that: \[ H^i(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathbb{Z}) \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;} H^i(X, \mathbb{Z}) \quad \text{for } i = 1, 2. \] In particular, \(H^1(X, \mathbb{Z}) = 0\) and \(H^2(X, \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z} \cdot c_1(\mathcal{O}_X(1))\). By Hodge decomposition, \(H^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X) = 0\). From the exponential sheaf sequence: \[ 0 = H^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X) \longrightarrow \operatorname{Pic}(X) \xrightarrow{\;c_1\;} H^2(X, \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}, \] the first Chern class \(c_1\) gives an isomorphism \(\operatorname{Pic}(X) \cong \mathbb{Z} \cdot \mathcal{O}_X(1) \cong \operatorname{Pic}(\mathbb{P}^r)\).
